\section{Polarized cofactors and propagation}
\label{uni-sec:cofactor}

Uniform ellipticity is sufficient for the propagation of an extremum, but it is not necessary: the directions in which the cofactor is positive may already connect enough points to force constancy. For $C^2$ potentials, the quadratic form $Q$ in \eqref{uni-eq:Q} is continuous, and its polarization defines $Q^\sharp:T^*X\to TX$ by $\eta(Q^\sharp\xi)=Q(\xi,\eta)$. The \emph{reachable set} of a point consists of the endpoints of absolutely continuous curves starting there whose velocity lies in $\operatorname{im}Q^\sharp$ almost everywhere. The \emph{control orbit} of a point consists of the endpoints of finite concatenations of flow lines, starting there, of locally Lipschitz vector fields with values in $\operatorname{im}Q^\sharp$.

A positive weight $h$ satisfying $\ddc(hT)=0$ gives an energy identity that controls $\mathrm{d}w$ everywhere. Without such a weight, a degenerate maximum principle gives propagation only from the extremal sets. This distinction accounts for the different density assumptions on reachable sets and on orbits in the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}\label{uni-thm:cofactor-intro}\index{Cofactor!weighted energy and propagation}
Let $u,v\in C^2(X)$ satisfy $A,B\geq0$ and $A^n=B^n$, and put $w=u-v$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If there is a continuous $h>0$ such that
$\ddc(hT)=0$ in distributions, then
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{i}\,\partial w\wedge\bar\partial w\wedge T\equiv0.
  \label{uni-eq:energyzero}
\end{equation}
It follows that $w$ is constant if the set where $T$ is strictly positive
is dense, or if the reachable set of one point is dense.
\item Without the weight hypothesis, the sets where $w$ attains its maximum and its minimum
are invariant under the flow of every locally Lipschitz vector field with values
in $\operatorname{im}Q^\sharp$.  If every control orbit is dense,
then $w$ is constant.  For smooth data it is enough that smooth vector
fields with values in $\operatorname{im}Q^\sharp$, for example the columns of the symbol $a$ in \eqref{uni-eq:real-symbol} below, together with their iterated Lie brackets,
span $TX$ at every point.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Weighted energy}

Testing the weighted cofactor against $w^2$ produces a nonnegative gradient integral. Only distributional $\ddc$-closedness of $hT$ is needed: for a continuous $h>0$, the condition is
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:weightedclosed}
 \int_X\ddc\varphi\wedge hT=0\qquad(\varphi\in C^\infty(X,\mathbb R)).
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}[of \cref{uni-thm:cofactor-intro}(1)]
The product rule gives
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:w2}
 \ddc(w^2)=2w\ddc w+2\mathrm{d}w\wedge \dc w
          =2w\ddc w+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\pi}\partial w\wedge\bar\partial w.
\end{equation}
Approximate $w^2$ in $C^2$ by smooth functions $\varphi$ in
\eqref{uni-eq:weightedclosed}.  Since $hT$ is continuous and
$\ddc w\wedge T=0$ by \eqref{uni-eq:linearization}, this gives
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:energy}
 0=\frac1\pi\int_X h\,\mathrm{i}\partial w\wedge\bar\partial w\wedge T.
\end{equation}
The continuous integrand is nonnegative, so it vanishes identically, which is \eqref{uni-eq:energyzero}.

By \eqref{uni-eq:Q}, \eqref{uni-eq:energyzero} says that $Q(\mathrm{d}w)=0$. For the nonnegative quadratic form $Q$,
this implies $Q(\mathrm{d}w,\eta)=0$ for every $\eta$.  Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:annihilate}
 \mathrm{d}w_x\bigl(Q_x^\sharp\eta\bigr)=0\qquad(x\in X,\ \eta\in T_x^*X).
\end{equation}
By the chain rule and \eqref{uni-eq:annihilate}, $w$ is constant along every absolutely continuous curve whose velocity lies in $\operatorname{im}Q^\sharp$ almost everywhere. Thus $w$ is constant on each reachable set, and by continuity one dense reachable set suffices.  If
instead $Q$ is positive definite on a dense set, \eqref{uni-eq:energyzero}
gives $\mathrm{d}w=0$ there, and hence everywhere.
\end{proof}

The argument uses only the test function $w^2$. For general test functions, integration by parts introduces derivatives of $hT$; the condition $\ddc(hT)=0$ alone does not make $L\varphi=(\ddc\varphi\wedge T)/\mathrm{d}V$ a symmetric operator in divergence form.

\subsection{The real symbol and exterior-ball propagation}

To propagate an extremum without a weight, we write the linearized operator in real coordinates. Its symbol is continuous and semipositive. At a point where a ball outside the maximum set touches it, the normal direction must lie in the kernel of the symbol; this forces the maximum set to be invariant under the flows of vector fields with values in the range of the symbol.

In a holomorphic chart $z_j=x_j+\mathrm{i} y_j$, let $C_{\rm cf}$ be the
Hermitian matrix characterized by
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:Ccf}
 \frac{\bigl(\mathrm{i}\sum H_{j\bar k}\mathrm{d}z_j\wedge \mathrm{d}\bar z_k\bigr)\wedge T}{\mathrm{d}V}
     =\operatorname{Tr}(C_{\rm cf}H)
\end{equation}
for every Hermitian matrix $H=(H_{j\bar k})$.  Positivity of mixed products makes
$C_{\rm cf}$ positive semidefinite.  Write
$C_{\rm cf}=P_{\rm cf}+\mathrm{i} Q_{\rm cf}$, where $P_{\rm cf}$ is real
symmetric and $Q_{\rm cf}$ is real skew-symmetric.  In the real coordinate
order $(x_1,\ldots,x_n,y_1,\ldots,y_n)$,
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:real-symbol}
 L\varphi:=\frac{\ddc\varphi\wedge T}{\mathrm{d}V}
       =\operatorname{Tr}(aD^2\varphi),\qquad
 a=\frac1{8\pi}
 \begin{pmatrix}P_{\rm cf}&Q_{\rm cf}\\-Q_{\rm cf}&P_{\rm cf}\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
Indeed
$\varphi_{j\bar k}=(\varphi_{x_jx_k}+\varphi_{y_jy_k}
+\mathrm{i}\varphi_{x_jy_k}-\mathrm{i}\varphi_{y_jx_k})/4$; inserting this expression
in \eqref{uni-eq:Ccf} proves \eqref{uni-eq:real-symbol}.  For real coordinate vectors $\xi,\eta\in\mathbb R^n$, moreover,
\[
 (\xi,\eta)^Ta(\xi,\eta)
    =\frac1{8\pi}(\xi-\mathrm{i}\eta)^*C_{\rm cf}(\xi-\mathrm{i}\eta)\geq0.
\]
Thus $a$ is a continuous positive semidefinite principal symbol, the operator $L$ has no first-order term in
holomorphic coordinates, and the range of $a$ is that of $Q^\sharp$.

An exterior-ball argument propagates the maximum for this operator.  Let $W\in C^2$
satisfy $LW\geq0$ in a chart, suppose $W\leq M$, and put
$K=\{W=M\}$.  If $z\in K$ is a point of $K$ nearest to a point $y\notin K$ of the chart, then
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:normalzero}
 (y-z)^Ta(z)(y-z)=0.
\end{equation}
To prove this, suppose the left side is positive, set
$r_0=|y-z|$, and shrink the tangent ball toward $z$:
$y'=z+\theta(y-z)$, $R=\theta r_0$, with $0<\theta<1$ small.
The closed ball $\overline{B(y',R)}$ lies in the chart and meets $K$
only at $z$.  This follows from the triangle inequality; equality in
$|k-y|\leq|k-y'|+|y'-y|\leq r_0$, for $k\in K$, forces $k=z$.
For large $\alpha>0$, the function
\[
 \psi(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha R^2}-\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha|x-y'|^2}
\]
satisfies
\[
 L\psi=2\alpha \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha|x-y'|^2}
   \bigl(\operatorname{tr}a-2\alpha(x-y')^Ta(x)(x-y')\bigr)<0
\]
in a neighborhood of $z$, because $(z-y')^Ta(z)(z-y')>0$ by assumption.  Choose a sufficiently small $r<R$, and
consider the lens $D=B(z,r)\cap B(y',R)$.  On the part of its boundary lying on the
sphere $\partial B(y',R)$, we have $\psi=0$ and $W-M\leq0$.  On the remaining
compact boundary portion, $W-M<0$, since the tangent ball meets $K$
only at $z$.  Hence, for sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$,
$W-M\leq\varepsilon\psi$ on $\partial D$.  At a positive interior
maximum of $W-M-\varepsilon\psi$, the Hessian would be nonpositive, whereas
$L(W-M-\varepsilon\psi)>0$; this is impossible since $a\geq0$.  Hence the
inequality $W-M\leq\varepsilon\psi$ holds in $D$.  Outside the ball $B(y',R)$ we have
$\psi\geq0$, so this inequality holds in a full neighborhood of $z$.
Since equality holds at $z$, the function $W-M-\varepsilon\psi$ has a local maximum at $z$, and therefore
\[
 0\leq LW(z)\leq\varepsilon L\psi(z)<0,
\]
proving \eqref{uni-eq:normalzero}.

Since $a\geq0$, its range is the orthogonal complement of its kernel, and
\eqref{uni-eq:normalzero} implies that $y-z$ lies in this kernel. Consequently
$(y-z)\cdot Z(z)=0$ for every vector field $Z$ with values in the range of $a$.  If $Z$ is locally
Lipschitz and $\gamma$ is one of its flow curves starting in $K$, let
$d(t)$ denote the squared distance from $\gamma(t)$ to $K$ in a sufficiently small
compact chart neighborhood.  Shortening the time interval ensures that
nearest points $z_t$ lie in the chart interior.  Write $D^+d(t)=\limsup_{h\downarrow0}(d(t+h)-d(t))/h$ for the upper right derivative. Holding such a nearest
point fixed when differentiating the distance from above gives
\begin{align*}
 D^+d(t)&\leq2(\gamma(t)-z_t)\cdot Z(\gamma(t))\\
 &=2(\gamma(t)-z_t)\cdot\bigl(Z(\gamma(t))-Z(z_t)\bigr)
 \leq2\operatorname{Lip}(Z)d(t).
\end{align*}
As $d(0)=0$, Gronwall's inequality gives $d(t)=0$.  The same argument
for $-Z$, followed by continuation, proves invariance under both signs
of time and under finite concatenations of such flows.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\begin{proof}[of \cref{uni-thm:cofactor-intro}(2)]
Apply the preceding argument to $W=w$ and to $W=-w$ in each chart;
both satisfy $LW=0$ by \eqref{uni-eq:linearization}.  The maximum set of $w$ is nonempty,
closed, and contains every control orbit starting in it.  If every orbit is dense,
this maximum set is all of $X$, proving constancy.  The argument for the
minimum set is identical.

It remains to prove the bracket criterion. Suppose that a family of smooth vector fields with values in $\operatorname{im}Q^\sharp$ is bracket
generating.  At $p$, let $\mathcal D_p$ be the span of the values of
all original fields pushed forward by finite compositions of their local
flows and inverse flows.  This distribution contains the original fields
and is invariant under their flows.  Differentiation of pushed-forward
fields along a flow shows that it contains their nested Lie brackets;
finite-dimensional subspaces are closed, so the derivative remains in
$\mathcal D_p$.  Induction and the Jacobi identity give every iterated
bracket.  The assumed rank condition therefore gives
$\mathcal D_p=T_pX$.

Choose $2n$ such pushed-forward fields independent at $p$.  The flow
of each is a conjugate of an original flow and hence preserves its orbit.
The map obtained by successively flowing for $2n$ small times has
invertible derivative at zero.  The inverse function theorem puts a
neighborhood of $p$ in its orbit.  All orbits are open, and their
partition of connected $X$ has only one member.  This proves the
H\"ormander-type sufficient condition; it requires neither a constant-rank hypothesis
nor a smooth matrix square root of $a$.  In particular, the columns of a smooth
symbol $a$ may be used as the original vector fields.
\end{proof}

For $C^{2,1}$ potentials, the columns of $a$ are locally Lipschitz, so the orbit criterion still applies to them. The bracket criterion requires smooth vector fields.
