\extrachap{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

The singularities of a plurisubharmonic potential influence its Monge--Amp\`ere measure, the holomorphic functions integrable against its weight, and the geometry of the positive current it represents. On a compact K\"ahler manifold these relationships are organized by a closed background form. Closedness makes integration by parts compatible with positivity: it conserves the mass of bounded potentials, gives comparison principles, and produces an energy whose derivative is the Monge--Amp\`ere measure. Much of the global theory of singularities depends on this interaction between local analysis and global integration.

This book asks how these relationships change for Hermitian backgrounds. Its starting point is the theory of singularities and $\mathcal I$-goodness developed in \emph{Singularities in global pluripotential theory} \cite{SGPT}. The aim is to understand which information about a singularity can still be recovered from envelopes, measures and holomorphic sections when closedness is unavailable. Monge--Amp\`ere equations and Gauduchon geometry enter this question together: the equations construct potentials with controlled measures, while Gauduchon metrics supply conserved linear quantities where the nonlinear mass can vary.

\section*{The background form and the ambient manifold}

Two changes from the K\"ahler setting must be distinguished. A non-K\"ahler manifold can carry closed real $(1,1)$-forms and positive closed currents; their local potential theory remains available. Conversely, a nonclosed Hermitian background can be chosen on a K\"ahler manifold, and its torsion already changes the Monge--Amp\`ere calculus. Many of the difficulties studied here concern the chosen background, rather than the absence of any K\"ahler metric on the manifold. Global geometric restrictions enter separately, through the existence of closed positive classes, modifications and holomorphic sections.

For a smooth real $(1,1)$-form $\theta$ and a $\theta$-plurisubharmonic potential $u$, write $\theta_u=\theta+\ddc u$. When $\theta$ is closed, locally it is a complex Hessian, so $\theta_u$ is locally the Hessian of a plurisubharmonic function. For a general $\theta$, a smooth local correction still makes a $\theta$-plurisubharmonic potential plurisubharmonic, but a smooth remainder remains in the current. This is enough to retain compactness, regularization, multiplier ideals and the local construction of bounded products. It does not remove derivatives of the background from products or integration by parts. The general plurisubharmonic tools are therefore developed together in \cref{chap:framework}, before their global consequences are separated according to the background.

\section*{Mass, comparison and energy}

For a K\"ahler form $\omega$ and a smooth admissible potential $u$, Stokes' theorem gives
\[
 \int_X(\omega+\ddc u)^n=\int_X\omega^n.
\]
The same identity holds for bounded $\omega$-plurisubharmonic potentials. Singular potentials require the non-pluripolar product, and their mass can be smaller; even in the K\"ahler setting the bounded-potential identity must not be extended to arbitrary singularities. In a nonclosed background, the identity can already fail for smooth potentials. Its defect is explicit:
\[
 \int_X(\omega+\ddc u)^n-\int_X\omega^n
 =\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom nk
   \int_X u(\ddc u)^{k-1}\wedge\ddc(\omega^{n-k}).
\]
Thus a bounded change of potential can change the mass without changing its ordinary singularity type. \Cref{chap:mass} derives this formula and characterizes mass invariance by the vanishing of $\ddc(\omega^j)$ for $1\leq j\leq n-1$.

The consequences go beyond an error term in an identity. For the standard Hopf threefold, masses of smooth admissible potentials are unbounded above, although their infimum is positive. A different compact threefold has a balanced metric with finite upper mass and admits smooth admissible potentials whose masses tend to zero. These constructions, in \cref{chap:hopf,sec:vanishing-lower-mass,sec:lower-mass-metric-geometry}, separate an upper mass bound from a positive lower mass bound. Neither can be imposed silently on the general theory. The global comparison inequality familiar from K\"ahler geometry would force mass invariance; in its place, local and modified comparison estimates must retain the terms caused by torsion.

Energy imposes a stronger requirement than constant mass. On the space of smooth admissible potentials, consider the one-form
\[
 \alpha_u(v)=\int_X v(\omega+\ddc u)^n.
\]
For a K\"ahler background it is the differential of the Monge--Amp\`ere energy. In dimension at least two, the existence of a primitive even on a neighborhood of the zero potential forces $\mathrm d\omega=0$ (\cref{thm:raw-primitive}). A Gauduchon metric on a non-K\"ahler surface conserves total mass but does not supply this primitive. Consequently the K\"ahler variational argument cannot be recovered from mass conservation alone. The same distinction reappears in interpolation: Perron envelopes and Legendre duality remain meaningful, while the relation between the homogeneous product equation and a metric geodesic equation changes in a nonclosed background (\cref{chap:hermitian-rays,chap:hermitian-testcurves}).

\section*{Linear control and nonlinear equations}

A Gauduchon metric $\gamma$ supplies a replacement for one part of the lost conservation law. Its defining equation $\ddc(\gamma^{n-1})=0$ gives
\[
 \int_X(\theta+\ddc u)\wedge\gamma^{n-1}
 =\int_X\theta\wedge\gamma^{n-1}.
\]
The trace measure $(\theta+\ddc u)\wedge\gamma^{n-1}$ determines $u$ up to a constant. In a fixed background, weak convergence of these measures is equivalent to $L^1$ convergence of mean-normalized potentials (\cref{chap:compactness}). Its conserved total mass, however, does not bound the nonlinear Monge--Amp\`ere mass. The examples in \cref{chap:products} retain strong convergence of currents and traces while their nonlinear masses are infinite. To treat these potentials, the book constructs extended non-pluripolar measures and finite weighted measures. For nonpositive potentials, damping near the pole set restores continuity under convergence in capacity; passing back to undamped measures requires control of the tails (\cref{chap:damped-measures}).

The loss of mass conservation also changes what it means to prescribe a volume form. For smooth positive data, the Hermitian equation has the form
\[
 (\omega+\ddc u)^n=c\,\mathrm e^F\,\mathrm dV,
 \qquad \omega+\ddc u>0,\qquad \sup_Xu=0,
\]
and both $u$ and $c>0$ are unknown. For a K\"ahler form, integration determines $c=\int_X\omega^n/\int_X\mathrm e^F\,\mathrm dV$ before the equation is solved. In general the numerator would be the mass of the unknown solution. The equation determines a unique smooth pair, and its adjoint Gauduchon probability describes the variation of $\log c$ with the density. This turns normalization into a geometric functional rather than merely a fixed integral constraint.

Nonnegative, nonzero $L^p$ densities, $p>1$, still admit continuous solutions with a unique positive scalar. Potential uniqueness is more delicate: a smooth density with zeros can admit distinct normalized smooth solutions in a Hermitian background. For smooth nonnegative data, connectedness and density of the positive set recover uniqueness among bounded solutions, as proved in \cref{thm:connected-positive-uniqueness}. Scalar uniqueness and selection by exponential approximation remain separate from these potential-uniqueness results. Prescribing a singularity type requires further control of the reference potential, developed in \cref{chap:prescribed-equations}; arbitrary finite non-pluripolar measures are not universally solvable on Hermitian manifolds.

The scalar functional leads back to Gauduchon geometry. Its supporting probabilities extend smooth adjoint measures, and Gauduchon test forms define a finite variational volume whose value on a positive background is its equation constant. This construction remains finite even where upper Monge--Amp\`ere mass is infinite. Its extension to boundary classes, weak dual attainment and degeneration are studied in \cref{chap:gauduchon-variational-volume}. The invariant has a precise limit as a description of singularities: distinct divisorial types can have the same entire scalar functional and the same supporting probabilities. Stability of these data does not by itself recover the potential or its ordinary type (\cref{chap:normalization}).

\section*{Envelopes and the information in a singularity}

Ordinary singularity type records a potential up to bounded difference. Multiplier ideals record the integrability of holomorphic functions against all positive multiples of its weight. These are different kinds of information, and the corresponding envelopes need not agree. For positive-mass singularities in a closed big class on a compact K\"ahler manifold, $\mathcal I$-goodness links equality of the ordinary and ideal envelopes to a mass identity and to approximation by analytic singularities in the singularity topology. The Hermitian problem is to recover such links with quantities that retain their meaning when mass varies or becomes infinite.

The two envelopes themselves survive for smooth nonclosed backgrounds. In a positive Hermitian background, correcting the curvature error keeps dyadic analytic approximants in the original potential space. Their envelopes decrease to the ideal envelope. Combining convergence of both envelopes with controlled ideal inclusions and divisorial total variation gives a uniform topology whose analytic closure consists exactly of the classes with equal envelopes (\cref{thm:ordinary-topology-analytic-closure}). This applies even to classes with infinite-mass representatives; their canonical envelopes still have finite mass. The topology does not separate all ordinary classes. On curve models it is weaker than the complete polar-variation topology below, since diffuse polar mass can concentrate in an $L^1$ limit. Keeping the zero obstacle fixed also makes this topology change under smooth changes of the background, by \cref{prop:ordinary-topology-gauge-failure}. This failure already occurs on curves, where the backgrounds are closed; it comes from the lost polar curvature rather than torsion. Controlling all smooth obstacles restores invariance under smooth changes of background. Its analytic closure is characterized by equality of both envelopes for every obstacle, but the curve topology is still incomplete and weaker than polar total variation (\cref{thm:all-obstacle-analytic-closure,prop:curve-all-obstacle-incomplete}).

To extract an ordinary-type invariant from mass, one can take its infimum and supremum over all representatives of the type. The resulting lower and upper volumes need not coincide. For a smooth big background under bounded mass, volume comparison gives a completely metrizable topology on ordinary models of positive lower volume, although the comparison itself need not satisfy the triangle inequality. The complete metric keeps its Cauchy sequences away from zero lower volume. On a compact curve with a fixed positive background, a different complete metric includes the zero-volume boundary: it is exactly total variation of the polar part of the curvature measure (\cref{thm:positive-model-complete-metric,thm:curve-model-volume-distance,cor:curve-model-completeness}). Weak convergence of the full currents loses information retained by this metric.

Equations for general finite non-pluripolar measures require their own hypotheses. In a semipositive big background, positive lower mass and a model reference admitting a competitor in a smaller background give solutions in the relative envelope class. For an arbitrary smooth big background, the corresponding construction uses bounded mass and positive lower volume of the model reference. These two regimes form \cref{chap:relative-finite-measures,chap:bounded-mass-equations}; bounded difference from the reference requires a separate argument. Together with volume comparison they occupy \cref{part:mass-bounds}, while the earlier chapters develop the constructions available without these uniform mass assumptions.

\section*{Poles and metric geometry}

A logarithmic pole can either be prescribed or forced by the background. In \cref{sec:prescribed}, resolving a prescribed pole leaves a strictly positive residual form, so a bounded Hermitian equation determines the remaining potential. Its bounded residual need not descend, even when the full logarithmic potential does. In the blowup backgrounds of \cref{sec:blowups}, the exceptional divisor instead occurs in every positive current. Its coefficient belongs to the minimal type, and removing it leaves a current pulled back from the target. The same logarithmic appearance thus conceals different constraints on the residual and its behavior along exceptional fibers.

The metric defined by a positive current off its singular set contains further information. A H\"older potential controls the local trace mass and bounds path distance by the square root of its H\"older modulus; if the current is smooth and strictly positive away from a nonempty analytic set, that set lies at finite completion distance (\cref{thm:holder-current-distance}). Boundedness alone does not give this conclusion. The prescribed radial densities in \cref{sec:radial} separate the integral controlling the potential from the integral controlling radial length, and produce continuous minimal residuals with complete regular ends. Density zeros also constrain smoothness of exceptional residuals, but their vanishing order alone does not determine regularity or distance (\cref{sec:geometric-zeros}). The radial examples already occur in closed K\"ahler classes, so their completeness issue is separate from the torsion effects above.

\section*{Restriction, sections and geometry}

The geometric questions ask how much singularity information survives restriction or passage to another model. Ordinary restriction may lose meaning on a subspace contained in the pole set, whereas restricted ideals and trace envelopes can still encode data there. Divisorial currents give concrete representatives on modifications. Compatibility of numerical classes, compatibility of envelopes and realization by a single ordinary type are separate steps; \cref{chap:trace-data,chap:divisorial-models,chap:nonclosed-bclasses,chap:hermitian-envelope-systems} develops their relationships.

Holomorphic sections introduce another distinction between the background and the ambient manifold. The curvature of a line bundle is closed, even when an auxiliary Hermitian form is not. Thus section counts cannot be expected to measure arbitrary Hermitian Monge--Amp\`ere mass. Gauduchon degrees bound sections and flag valuations, while fibrations explain cases in which their growth and equilibrium live on a lower-dimensional base. Partial kernels connect this algebraic information with the potentials selected by their weights. Closed Bott--Chern classes and locally conformally K\"ahler structures then provide geometric settings with additional differential constraints, rather than consequences valid for every nonclosed background.

The exposition assumes familiarity with complex manifolds, differential forms and currents. \Cref{chap:framework} supplies the general plurisubharmonic prerequisites. The mass and energy calculations that follow explain why the later constructions are needed; the Hopf and elliptic examples test the hypotheses throughout the book. Readers chiefly interested in equations can then follow \cref{chap:smooth-equations,chap:weak-equations,chap:degenerate-uniqueness,chap:prescribed-equations}, while the envelope theory leads into singularities, traces and sections. The appendix extends descent and resolution-independent measures to unibranch compact spaces and treats equations on projective cones.

The remaining questions are formulated in \cref{chap:research-program}. The final historical chapter identifies paper sources and the precise dependencies of the arguments; book references also appear where they are used.
