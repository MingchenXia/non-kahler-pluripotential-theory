\extrachap{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

For a fixed K\"ahler class, the Monge--Amp\`ere mass of a bounded potential is independent of the potential. This consequence of Stokes' theorem allows mass to be used in the study of singularities: one can compare the mass lost at poles with the growth of the potential and the integrability of holomorphic functions against its weight. For a Hermitian background, the mass can change even among smooth potentials. The same singularity type can then have representatives with different masses.

We study singularities in this setting, taking the theory developed in \emph{Singularities in global pluripotential theory} \cite{SGPT} as a starting point. The central question is how to relate ordinary singularity type, multiplier ideals and Monge--Amp\`ere measures when the background is not closed. Envelopes remain useful, but the mass identities and variational arguments that connect them in K\"ahler theory need to be reconsidered. Some arguments admit a replacement; others require additional hypotheses or fail in explicit examples.

The distinction concerns the background as well as the manifold. A non-K\"ahler manifold may carry closed positive currents, whereas a K\"ahler manifold may be equipped with a nonclosed Hermitian form. We keep these two situations separate. Let $X$ be a connected compact complex manifold of dimension $n\geq1$, and let $\theta$ be a smooth real $(1,1)$-form on $X$. A $\theta$-plurisubharmonic potential $u$ has positive current $\theta_u=\theta+\ddc u$. If $\theta$ is closed, this current is locally a complex Hessian. For a general background, a smooth local correction makes $u$ plurisubharmonic, leaving a smooth remainder in the current. Local potential theory still supplies compactness, regularization and multiplier ideals. The difference appears when these local constructions are integrated over $X$.

\section*{Closedness, mass and energy}

Let $\omega$ be a Hermitian form on $X$. If it is K\"ahler, every smooth real potential $u$ with $\omega+\ddc u>0$ satisfies
\[
 \int_X(\omega+\ddc u)^n=\int_X\omega^n.
\]
The identity extends to bounded $\omega$-plurisubharmonic potentials. For singular potentials, the non-pluripolar product can have smaller total mass. Closedness provides the fixed total mass against which this loss is measured.

For a smooth potential in a Hermitian background, integration by parts instead gives
\[
 \int_X(\omega+\ddc u)^n-\int_X\omega^n
 =\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom nk
   \int_X u(\ddc u)^{k-1}\wedge\ddc(\omega^{n-k}).
\]
Thus the failure of mass conservation is already visible before singularities occur. \Cref{chap:mass} derives this formula and shows that mass invariance is equivalent to the vanishing of $\ddc(\omega^j)$ for $1\leq j\leq n-1$. The usual global comparison inequality would force mass invariance, so comparison estimates for a general Hermitian form must account for the derivatives of the background.

Upper and lower mass bounds are separate questions. On the standard Hopf threefold, smooth admissible potentials have unbounded masses, although their infimum is positive. Another compact threefold carries a balanced metric with finite upper mass and smooth admissible potentials whose masses tend to zero. These examples, in \cref{chap:hopf,sec:vanishing-lower-mass}, explain why a general Hermitian theory cannot assume either bound. The later mass-bound part develops the theory under explicit upper and lower bounds.

Constant mass does not suffice to recover the K\"ahler energy. At a smooth admissible potential $u$, the Monge--Amp\`ere operator defines the one-form
\[
 \alpha_u(v)=\int_X v(\omega+\ddc u)^n,
\]
where $v$ is a smooth real tangent direction. For a K\"ahler form this is the differential of the Monge--Amp\`ere energy. In dimension at least two, a primitive on even a neighborhood of the zero potential forces $\mathrm d\omega=0$, by \cref{thm:raw-primitive}. A Gauduchon metric on a non-K\"ahler surface conserves mass but has no such primitive. This is one reason to distinguish constructions using envelopes from arguments depending on an energy functional. Perron interpolation and Legendre duality remain available, but their relation to metric geodesics changes with the background.

A linear conservation law does survive. For a Gauduchon form $\gamma$, the equation $\ddc(\gamma^{n-1})=0$ gives
\[
 \int_X(\theta+\ddc u)\wedge\gamma^{n-1}
 =\int_X\theta\wedge\gamma^{n-1}.
\]
The trace measure on the left determines $u$ up to a constant. Fix a smooth positive volume form $\mathrm dV$ on $X$. With the normalization $\int_Xu\,\mathrm dV=0$, weak convergence of trace measures is equivalent to $L^1$ convergence of potentials in a fixed background, as proved in \cref{chap:compactness}. The trace therefore controls the potential even when it does not control the nonlinear mass. Potentials with finite trace can have infinite Monge--Amp\`ere mass. We construct extended non-pluripolar measures and finite damped measures to handle them; removal of the damping requires a separate estimate on the tails near the pole set.

\section*{Equations and singularity types}

The changing mass affects the normalization of an equation. Given a smooth real function $F$ on $X$, we seek a smooth pair $(u,c)$ satisfying
\[
 (\omega+\ddc u)^n=c\,\mathrm e^F\,\mathrm dV,
 \qquad \omega+\ddc u>0,\qquad \sup_Xu=0.
\]
Here $c>0$ is part of the unknown. In the K\"ahler case, integration fixes it before solving for $u$. In the Hermitian case, the integral on the left depends on $u$. The equation nevertheless determines a unique smooth pair. Its linearization produces an adjoint Gauduchon probability measure, which describes how $\log c$ changes with the density. This dependence leads to a variational volume defined by Gauduchon test forms. The variational volume remains finite even on manifolds with unbounded upper Monge--Amp\`ere mass.

For nonnegative, nonzero $L^p$ densities with $p>1$, continuous solutions still exist with a unique positive scalar. Uniqueness of the potential requires more care. A smooth density with zeros can admit distinct normalized smooth solutions; for smooth nonnegative data, connectedness and density of the positive set recover uniqueness among bounded solutions, by \cref{thm:connected-positive-uniqueness}. We retain the distinction between uniqueness of the scalar, uniqueness of the potential and selection by an approximation procedure. Equations with a prescribed singularity type introduce further conditions on the reference potential.

The scalar does not recover all the singularity data. Distinct divisorial types can give the same entire normalization functional and the same supporting Gauduchon probabilities, as shown in \cref{chap:normalization}. To retain the distinction, we turn to envelopes.

Ordinary singularity type identifies potentials whose difference is bounded. Multiplier ideals instead record which holomorphic functions are locally integrable against positive multiples of the weight. The ordinary envelope $P_\theta[u]$ and the multiplier-ideal envelope $P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$ compare these two descriptions. In a closed big class on a compact K\"ahler manifold, $\mathcal I$-goodness relates equality of the envelopes to a mass identity and analytic approximation, under the positive-mass hypotheses. We examine which parts of that relation persist for a nonclosed background.

Analytic approximation remains possible in a positive Hermitian background. Correcting curvature errors keeps dyadic approximants in the original potential space, and their envelopes decrease to the ideal envelope. A uniform topology on ordinary classes then characterizes the analytic closure by equality of the two envelopes, even for classes with infinite-mass representatives (\cref{thm:ordinary-topology-analytic-closure}). This topology can identify distinct ordinary classes. It also changes under a smooth gauge change $\theta\mapsto\theta+\ddc\chi$ if the zero obstacle is kept fixed. Including all smooth obstacles restores invariance under this change, with the potential transformed by $u\mapsto u-\chi$. In a smooth big background with bounded mass and positive lower ideal volume, zero-obstacle equality implies equality for all smooth obstacles. The same implication holds without these volume conditions on curves and on the specified product constructions (\cref{prop:conditional-i-characterization,cor:curve-arbitrary-background-criterion,cor:general-auxiliary-all-obstacle-criterion}). The general zero-volume implication remains open.

To attach a volume to an ordinary type, we take the infimum and supremum of mass over all its representatives. These lower and upper singularity volumes need not agree. Under bounded mass in a smooth big background, volume comparison gives a completely metrizable topology on ordinary models of positive lower volume. At zero volume, the same comparison can fail to distinguish different models. On a curve, total variation of polar curvature gives a stronger complete metric that includes the zero-volume boundary. Its ideal subspace is the closure of the analytic models. The curve construction also supplies complete zero-volume model loci on higher-dimensional products without an ambient mass bound, by \cref{prop:general-auxiliary-product-metric}. Extending this metric beyond those loci is one of the questions left by the book.

General finite non-pluripolar measures require their own existence hypotheses. The final mass-bound part treats two regimes: semipositive big backgrounds with positive lower mass and a model reference admitting a competitor in a smaller background, and arbitrary smooth big backgrounds with bounded mass and positive lower volume of the model reference. The resulting solution lies in a relative envelope class; a bounded difference from the reference requires a further argument. The envelope and trace constructions described above provide the tools for both regimes.

\section*{Geometric constructions}

Logarithmic poles give concrete tests of the singularity theory. A prescribed pole can leave a strictly positive residual form after resolution, allowing a bounded equation to determine the residual potential. A divisor forced by the background behaves differently: its coefficient belongs to the minimal type, and removing it can force the remaining current to descend. The residual in a prescribed-pole construction need not have this descent property. The constructions in \cref{sec:prescribed,sec:blowups} make the distinction explicit.

The metric defined by a current on its regular set raises a further question. A H\"older potential gives local trace estimates and finite distance to a nonempty analytic boundary when the current is smooth and strictly positive off that boundary (\cref{thm:holder-current-distance}). A bounded potential alone gives no such conclusion. Radial examples have continuous minimal residuals and complete regular ends, already in closed K\"ahler classes. Here the integral controlling the potential differs from the integral controlling path length. These examples concern the strength of regularity assumptions rather than the loss of closedness.

Restriction and modification provide other ways to examine a singularity. A potential may be identically minus infinity on the subspace where one wishes to restrict it, while restricted ideals and trace envelopes still carry data there. Realizing those data by a current or by one ordinary singularity type requires compatibility beyond that of numerical classes. We develop trace envelopes and divisorial representatives with this problem in view.

Holomorphic sections connect singularities with geometry, but their curvature is closed. Section counts therefore cannot measure arbitrary Hermitian Monge--Amp\`ere mass. Gauduchon degrees control sections and flag valuations, and fibrations explain examples whose section growth and equilibrium measures live on a lower-dimensional base. Partial kernels relate these section spaces to envelopes selected by singular weights. Closed Bott--Chern classes and locally conformally K\"ahler structures provide additional geometric settings in which the differential constraints can be used more precisely.

\section*{Reading the book}

We assume familiarity with complex manifolds, differential forms and currents. \Cref{chap:framework} develops the plurisubharmonic tools used throughout. On a first reading, the maximum principle, normalized compactness and exponential integrability are the main starting points; the longer approximation and descent arguments can be consulted when needed. The mass and energy calculations explain the obstructions described above, and Gauduchon traces, non-pluripolar products and the Hopf construction complete the foundations.

From there, the equation chapters lead to prescribed singularities, while the envelope chapters lead to interpolation, restriction and holomorphic sections. The later mass-bound part develops volume comparison and general-measure equations under its explicit hypotheses. The appendix treats descent, measures and equations on compact unibranch spaces, including projective cones. Open questions are collected in \cref{chap:research-program}; the historical chapter records paper sources and exact dependencies. Book references also appear where they are used.
