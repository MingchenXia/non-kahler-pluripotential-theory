\extrachap{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}

In a fixed K\"ahler class, all bounded potentials have the same Monge--Amp\`ere mass. This consequence of Stokes' theorem makes mass a tool for studying singularities: the mass lost at the poles of a potential can be compared with its growth and with the integrability of holomorphic functions against its exponential weight. For a Hermitian background, the mass can change even among smooth potentials, so one singularity type can have representatives of different masses.

This book studies singularities in the Hermitian setting, taking the theory developed in \emph{Singularities in global pluripotential theory} \cite{SGPT} as its starting point. The central question is how ordinary singularity types, multiplier ideals and Monge--Amp\`ere measures are related when the background is not closed. Envelopes remain useful, but the mass identities and variational arguments that connect these notions in K\"ahler theory have to be reconsidered: some arguments admit a replacement, while others require additional hypotheses or fail in explicit examples.

Two departures from K\"ahler geometry must be distinguished, one concerning the manifold and one concerning the background form. A non-K\"ahler manifold may carry closed positive currents, whereas a K\"ahler manifold may be equipped with a nonclosed Hermitian form; we keep these two situations separate. Let $X$ be a connected compact complex manifold of dimension $n\geq1$, and let $\theta$ be a smooth real $(1,1)$-form on $X$. A function $u$ is $\theta$-plurisubharmonic if it is locally the sum of a plurisubharmonic function and a smooth function and the current $\theta_u=\theta+\ddc u$ is positive; the normalization of $\ddc$ is fixed in the Conventions that follow this Introduction. If $\theta$ is closed, this current is locally a complex Hessian. For a general background, a smooth local correction still makes $u$ plurisubharmonic, but it leaves a smooth remainder in the current. Local potential theory therefore still provides compactness, regularization and multiplier ideals; the difference appears when one integrates over $X$.

\section*{Closedness, mass and energy}

Let $\omega$ be a Hermitian form on $X$, that is, a smooth strictly positive $(1,1)$-form. If $\omega$ is K\"ahler, every smooth real potential $u$ with $\omega+\ddc u>0$ satisfies
\[
 \int_X(\omega+\ddc u)^n=\int_X\omega^n.
\]
The identity extends to bounded $\omega$-plurisubharmonic potentials. For singular potentials, the non-pluripolar product can have smaller total mass, and closedness provides the fixed total mass against which this loss is measured.

If $\omega$ is only Hermitian, integration by parts gives instead, for every smooth potential $u$,
\[
 \int_X(\omega+\ddc u)^n-\int_X\omega^n
 =\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom nk
   \int_X u(\ddc u)^{k-1}\wedge\ddc(\omega^{n-k}).
\]
Mass conservation can thus fail before any singularity appears. \Cref{chap:mass} derives this formula and shows that mass invariance is equivalent to the vanishing of $\ddc(\omega^j)$ for $1\leq j\leq n-1$. Since the usual global comparison inequality would force mass invariance, comparison estimates for a general Hermitian form must take the derivatives of the background into account.

Upper and lower bounds for the mass are separate questions. Call a smooth potential $u$ \emph{admissible} if $\omega+\ddc u>0$. On the standard Hopf threefold, the masses of admissible potentials are unbounded, although their infimum is positive. Another compact threefold carries a balanced metric whose admissible potentials have bounded masses but include sequences with masses tending to zero. These examples, given in \cref{chap:hopf,sec:vanishing-lower-mass}, show that a general Hermitian theory can assume neither bound. \Cref{part:mass-bounds} develops the theory under explicit upper and lower bounds.

Even constant mass does not recover the K\"ahler energy. At an admissible potential $u$, the Monge--Amp\`ere operator defines the one-form
\[
 \alpha_u(v)=\int_X v(\omega+\ddc u)^n,
\]
where the tangent direction $v$ is a smooth real function. For a K\"ahler form, this is the differential of the Monge--Amp\`ere energy. In dimension at least two, however, a primitive of this one-form even on a neighborhood of the zero potential forces $\mathrm d\omega=0$, by \cref{thm:raw-primitive}. On a non-K\"ahler surface, a Gauduchon metric, that is, a Hermitian form $\gamma$ with $\ddc(\gamma^{n-1})=0$, conserves mass but has no such primitive. This is one reason to distinguish constructions by envelopes from arguments that depend on an energy functional. Perron interpolation and Legendre duality remain available, but their relation to metric geodesics changes with the background.

A linear conservation law does survive. For a Gauduchon form $\gamma$, the equation $\ddc(\gamma^{n-1})=0$ gives, for every $\theta$-plurisubharmonic $u$,
\[
 \int_X(\theta+\ddc u)\wedge\gamma^{n-1}
 =\int_X\theta\wedge\gamma^{n-1}.
\]
The trace measure on the left determines $u$ up to an additive constant. Fix a smooth positive volume form $\mathrm dV$ on $X$. For potentials in a fixed background normalized by $\int_Xu\,\mathrm dV=0$, weak convergence of the trace measures is equivalent to $L^1$ convergence of the potentials, as proved in \cref{chap:compactness}. The trace therefore controls the potential even when it does not control the nonlinear mass: every potential has finite trace mass, but its Monge--Amp\`ere mass can be infinite. To handle such potentials we construct extended non-pluripolar measures and finite damped measures; removing the damping requires a separate estimate of the tails near the pole set.

\section*{Equations and singularity types}

Since the mass varies, the normalization of a Monge--Amp\`ere equation becomes part of the problem. Given a smooth real function $F$ on $X$, we seek a smooth pair $(u,c)$ satisfying
\[
 (\omega+\ddc u)^n=c\,\mathrm e^F\,\mathrm dV,
 \qquad \omega+\ddc u>0,\qquad \sup_Xu=0.
\]
Here the constant $c>0$ is part of the unknown. In the K\"ahler case, integrating the equation determines $c$ before one solves for $u$; in the Hermitian case, the integral of the left-hand side depends on $u$. The equation nevertheless has a unique smooth solution $(u,c)$. Its linearization produces an adjoint Gauduchon probability measure, which describes how $\log c$ changes with the density. This dependence leads to a variational volume defined by Gauduchon test forms, which remains finite even on manifolds whose upper Monge--Amp\`ere mass is unbounded. A projective example has a nef nonclosed background $\theta$ that has positive variational volume but is not big, that is, admits no $\theta$-plurisubharmonic $\rho$ with $\theta+\ddc\rho\geq\delta\omega$ for some $\delta>0$ (\cref{thm:radial-positive-volume-nonbig}). Up to a smooth change of representative, the same background occurs at the finite maximal time of a Chern--Ricci flow (\cref{thm:actual-flow-positive-volume-nonbig}). Positive variational volume alone therefore does not provide the bigness needed to apply geometric convergence theorems.

When $\mathrm e^F$ is replaced by a nonnegative density in $L^p(X,\mathrm dV)$, $p>1$, that is not almost everywhere zero, continuous solutions still exist and the positive constant $c$ is still unique. Uniqueness of the potential requires more care. A smooth density with zeros can admit distinct normalized smooth solutions. For a smooth nonnegative density whose positive set is connected and dense, however, bounded solutions are unique up to an additive constant, by \cref{thm:connected-positive-uniqueness}. We therefore keep uniqueness of the constant, uniqueness of the potential and selection by an approximation procedure separate. Equations with a prescribed singularity type impose further conditions on the reference potential.

For such equations, the constant $c$ depends on the density through a normalization functional, and this functional does not recover all the singularity data: distinct divisorial types can have the same normalization functional and the same supporting Gauduchon probabilities, as shown in \cref{chap:normalization}. To distinguish such types, we turn to envelopes.

Ordinary singularity type identifies potentials whose difference is bounded. Multiplier ideals instead record which holomorphic functions are locally square integrable against the weights $\mathrm e^{-ku}$, $k>0$. Two envelopes compare these descriptions. The \emph{ordinary envelope} $P_\theta[u]$ is the upper semicontinuous regularization of the supremum of all $\theta$-plurisubharmonic $h\leq0$ with $h\leq u+C$ for some constant $C$. The \emph{multiplier-ideal envelope} $P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$ is defined in the same way from the $\theta$-plurisubharmonic $h\leq0$ that have the same multiplier ideals as $u$ at all weights $k>0$. In a closed big class on a compact K\"ahler manifold, and for potentials of positive mass, the equality $P_\theta[u]=P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$ characterizes $\mathcal I$-good singularities and is equivalent to a mass identity and to approximation by analytic singularities. We examine which parts of this relation persist for a nonclosed background.

Analytic approximation remains possible in a positive Hermitian background. After their curvature errors are corrected, dyadic approximants stay in the original space of potentials, and their envelopes decrease to the multiplier-ideal envelope. A uniform topology on ordinary singularity classes then characterizes the closure of the analytic classes by equality of the two envelopes, even for classes with infinite-mass representatives (\cref{thm:ordinary-topology-analytic-closure}). This topology can identify distinct ordinary classes. The envelopes above use the obstacle $0$, that is, the constraint $h\leq0$ on their competitors. If this obstacle is kept fixed, the topology changes under a smooth change of representative $\theta\mapsto\theta+\ddc\chi$, with the potentials transformed by $u\mapsto u-\chi$; including all smooth obstacles $h\leq\psi$ restores invariance under this change. In a smooth big background with bounded mass, if the multiplier-ideal envelope has positive lower volume in the sense of the next paragraph, equality of the envelopes at the obstacle $0$ implies equality at every smooth obstacle. The same implication holds without these volume conditions on curves and for certain product constructions (\cref{prop:conditional-i-characterization,cor:curve-arbitrary-background-criterion,cor:general-auxiliary-all-obstacle-criterion}). Whether it holds in general at zero volume remains open.

To attach a volume to an ordinary singularity type, we take the infimum and the supremum of the mass over all its representatives. These lower and upper singularity volumes need not agree. Call $u$ an \emph{ordinary model} if $P_\theta[u]=u$. In a smooth big background with bounded mass, volume comparison gives a completely metrizable topology on the ordinary models of positive lower volume. At zero volume, the same comparison can fail to distinguish different models.

On a curve, an ordinary model is determined by its polar curvature, the part of $\theta+\ddc u$ carried by the pole set of $u$. Total variation of polar curvature gives a complete metric that includes the models of zero volume, and in this metric the models with $P_\theta[u]=P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$ form the closure of the analytic models. In higher dimensions, residual mass in top degree can lie on a diffuse pole set while the polar parts of the curvature currents vanish on every modification; this happens already for K\"ahler forms. Even the whole residual measure, together with both volumes, can fail to determine the model: different weights in an anisotropic maximum give the same residual measure but a positive comparison distance (\cref{prop:equal-residual-anisotropic-models}). The curve construction nevertheless gives complete loci of zero-volume models on higher-dimensional products without an ambient mass bound, by \cref{prop:general-auxiliary-product-metric}. A general extension must keep the singularity information that these volumes and polar currents lose.

Equations whose right-hand side is a general finite non-pluripolar measure require their own existence hypotheses. \Cref{part:mass-bounds} treats two regimes: semipositive big backgrounds with positive lower mass, with a model reference that admits a competitor in a smaller background; and arbitrary smooth big backgrounds with bounded mass, with a model reference of positive lower volume. The resulting solution lies in a relative envelope class; that it differs from the reference by a bounded function requires a further argument. The envelope and trace constructions described above provide the tools for both regimes.

\section*{Geometric constructions}

Logarithmic poles provide concrete tests of the singularity theory. After resolution, a prescribed pole can leave a strictly positive residual form, and a bounded equation then determines the residual potential. A divisor forced by the background behaves differently: its coefficient belongs to the type of minimal singularities, and removing it can force the remaining current to descend. The residual in a prescribed-pole construction need not have this descent property. The constructions in \cref{sec:prescribed,sec:blowups} make the distinction explicit.

A current that is smooth and strictly positive off an analytic set defines a Hermitian metric there, and one may ask whether the analytic set lies at finite distance. If the potential is H\"older continuous, local trace estimates show that it does; in particular, the metric is incomplete when the analytic set is nonempty (\cref{thm:holder-current-distance}). Continuity of the potential alone gives no such conclusion: radial examples, already in closed K\"ahler classes, have continuous minimal residuals and complete regular ends. In these examples the integral that controls the potential differs from the integral that controls path length. They concern the strength of regularity assumptions rather than the loss of closedness.

Restriction and modification provide further ways to examine a singularity. A potential may be identically $-\infty$ on the subspace to which one wishes to restrict it, while restricted ideals and trace envelopes still carry data there. Realizing these data by a current or by a single ordinary singularity type requires compatibility conditions beyond those of numerical classes. We develop trace envelopes and divisorial representatives with this problem in view.

Holomorphic sections of line bundles connect singularities with geometry, but the curvature of a line bundle is closed. Counting sections therefore cannot measure an arbitrary Hermitian Monge--Amp\`ere mass. Gauduchon degrees control sections and flag valuations, and fibrations explain examples whose section growth and equilibrium measures live on a lower-dimensional base. Partial Bergman kernels relate these spaces of sections to envelopes defined by singular weights. Closed Bott--Chern classes and locally conformally K\"ahler structures provide further geometric settings in which the differential constraints can be used more precisely.

\section*{Reading the book}

We assume familiarity with complex manifolds, differential forms and currents. \Cref{chap:framework} develops the plurisubharmonic tools used throughout. On a first reading, the maximum principle, normalized compactness and exponential integrability are the main starting points; the longer approximation and descent arguments can be consulted when needed. The mass and energy calculations explain the obstructions described above, and Gauduchon traces, non-pluripolar products and the Hopf construction complete the foundations.

From there, the chapters on equations lead to prescribed singularities, while the chapters on envelopes lead to interpolation, restriction and holomorphic sections. \Cref{part:mass-bounds} develops volume comparison and equations with general measures under explicit mass hypotheses. The appendix treats descent, measures and equations on compact unibranch spaces, including projective cones. Open questions are collected in \cref{chap:research-program}, and \cref{chap:history} gives the sources in the literature and the precise dependencies on them. References to monographs also appear in the text where they are used.
