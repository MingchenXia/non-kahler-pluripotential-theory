\subsection{Varying the curve coefficients}
\label{subsec:weighted-curve-product-models}

The auxiliary coefficient in \cref{cor:nonclosed-separated-product-metric} does not change the metric on the specified locus. Changing the coefficients of its curve models has a different effect: a zero coefficient discards a coordinate, and the diameter depends on the remaining curve areas.

Keep the product $M=Y\times C_0\times Z$, its projections and the forms from that corollary. For $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_m)\in[0,\infty)^m$ and a smooth $f\geq0$ on $Y$, put
\[
 J(a)=\{i:a_i>0\},\qquad
 \beta_a=\sum_i a_i\pi_i^*\eta_i,\qquad
 \Theta_{a,f}=p^*\beta_a+(f\circ p)s^*\tau+r^*\kappa.
\]
For normalized ordinary models $q_i$ in the fixed curve backgrounds $\eta_i$, define
\[
 \Phi_a(q)=\Psi+\sum_{i\in J(a)}a_iq_i\circ\pi_i\circ p,
 \qquad \Psi=\psi\circ r.
\]
Terms with zero coefficient are omitted, including on their pole sets. Thus $\Phi_a$ depends only on the active coordinates. Write $\mathcal S_a$ for this locus of potentials and $q_{i,\mathcal I}=P_{\eta_i}[q_i]_{\mathcal I}$.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:weighted-product-model-projections}\index{Envelope!separated curve models}
For every $a$ and $f$ as above,
\[
 P_{\Theta_{a,f}}[\Phi_a(q)]=\Phi_a(q),\qquad
 P_{\Theta_{a,f}}[\Phi_a(q)]_{\mathcal I}=\Phi_a(q_{\mathcal I}).
\]
Both potentials have supremum zero. In particular, $\mathcal S_0=\{\Psi\}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $a_i>0$, scaling the ordinary competitors shows that $a_iq_i$ is an ordinary model for $a_i\eta_i$. Its polar curvature is $a_i\mu_i$, where $\mu_i$ is the polar curvature of $q_i$. Taking atomic parts and \cref{thm:curve-background-homothety} give its ideal model $a_iq_{i,\mathcal I}$.

Put $w_a=\sum_{i\in J(a)}a_iq_i\circ\pi_i$. If there are at least two active factors, \cref{lem:separated-curve-model-projections} makes the corresponding sum an ordinary model on their positive product background. One active factor uses the curve identity. Pullback over the inactive factors preserves this identity by \cref{prop:psh-descent-submersion}: every competitor in the pulled-back background descends, and its obstacle bound and ordinary order descend by surjectivity. With no active factors, the normalized model is zero by the maximum principle. Hence $P_{\beta_a}[w_a]=w_a$ in all cases.

On $B=Y\times C_0$, use $\beta_{a,f}=\pi^*\beta_a+(f\circ\pi)s_0^*\tau$. As in \cref{cor:nonclosed-separated-product-metric}, replace an ordinary competitor by its maximum with $w_a\circ\pi$ and restrict to each section $Y\times\{z\}$. This gives $P_{\beta_{a,f}}[w_a\circ\pi]=w_a\circ\pi$.

For the ideal identity take $g_a=\sum_{i\in J(a)}a_i g_i\circ\pi_i\circ\pi$, using the atomic Green potentials from \cref{lem:curve-atomic-ideal-model}. Scaling, addition and pullback preserve ideal equivalence by \cref{lem:hermitian-i-calculus,lem:submersion-multiplier-pullback}. The Hessian of $g_a$ is a closed finite-mass countable divisor current minus a smooth form, and $g_a$ is $\beta_{a,f}$-plurisubharmonic. It is ordinarily equivalent to $w_{a,\mathcal I}\circ\pi$, where $w_{a,\mathcal I}=\sum_{i\in J(a)}a_iq_{i,\mathcal I}\circ\pi_i$. By \cref{lem:divisorial-potential-domination}, every ideal competitor is an ordinary competitor for $g_a$ and is therefore bounded by $w_{a,\mathcal I}\circ\pi$. The latter is itself an ideal competitor, so the ideal envelope equals it. When $J(a)$ is empty both base envelopes are zero directly. The unit-divisor reduction of \cref{prop:fibration-obstacle-reduction} gives the upstairs identities. Independence of the factors gives normalization.
\end{proof}

For two tuples $q,\widetilde q$, let $\mu_i,\nu_i$ be their polar curvature measures in the fixed backgrounds $\eta_i$. Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-product-polar-metric}
 \rho_a(\Phi_a(q),\Phi_a(\widetilde q))
       =\sum_{i\in J(a)}a_i\|\mu_i-\nu_i\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.
\end{equation}
On the singleton $\mathcal S_0$ this distance is zero.

\Needspace{20\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:weighted-product-polar-metric}\index{Metric completeness!vanishing curve coefficients}\index{Model potential!weighted product collapse}
For every $a$, the locus $\mathcal S_a$ is a complete geodesic metric space for $\rho_a$, independently of $f$. Its ideal subspace is closed, geodesically convex and equal to the metric closure of its analytic models. Ideal projection is its unique nearest-point $1$-Lipschitz retraction, with distance
\[
 \rho_a\bigl(\Phi_a(q),\Phi_a(q_{\mathcal I})\bigr)
       =\sum_{i\in J(a)}a_i\mu_i^{\mathrm{na}}(C_i).
\]
Here $\mu_i^{\mathrm{na}}$ is the nonatomic part of $\mu_i$. The diameter is $2\sum_i a_iA_i$, and metric convergence implies $L^1(M)$ convergence for every smooth positive volume form. The top-volume comparison expression vanishes on this locus.

If $a,b$ have the same nonempty active set $J$, the correspondence $T_{ba}\Phi_a(q)=\Phi_b(q)$ is well defined, commutes with ideal projection and satisfies
\[
 \min_{i\in J}\frac{b_i}{a_i}\,\rho_a(u,v)
 \leq\rho_b(T_{ba}u,T_{ba}v)
 \leq\max_{i\in J}\frac{b_i}{a_i}\,\rho_a(u,v).
\]
For a fixed smooth positive volume form on $M$, there are constants $K_i$ independent of $a,b$ and of the normalized model tuple $q$ such that
\[
 \|\Phi_a(q)-\Phi_b(q)\|_{L^1(M)}
       \leq\sum_i K_i|a_i-b_i|.
\]
Consequently, as all $a_i$ tend to zero, the diameter tends to zero and every potential in $\mathcal S_a$ tends to $\Psi$ in $L^1(M)$, uniformly over the normalized model tuples.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Restriction to coordinate sections where the other potentials are finite determines every active $a_iq_i$ up to a constant. Normalization makes these constants zero. Thus \eqref{eq:weighted-product-polar-metric} is well defined and identifies $\mathcal S_a$ with the product of the active curve model spaces with their distances multiplied by $a_i$. There are no inactive coordinates to distinguish; the empty product is a singleton.

The curve completeness, geodesic and atomic-projection results in \cref{cor:curve-model-completeness,prop:curve-analytic-distance-defect} apply coordinate by coordinate. Their weighted distances add, proving the metric assertions and the distance formula. The bound on the diameter is attained by choosing polar measures $A_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $A_i\delta_{y_i}$ with $x_i\ne y_i$ on each active curve. Fiber integration and \cref{thm:curve-model-capacity-convergence} give $L^1$ convergence upstairs. The preceding lemma makes the ideal projection independent of $f$. Rank deficiency in the unit-divisor reduction also gives zero upper volume for each model and pairwise maximum, hence zero for the comparison expression.

For analytic density, approximate each actual atomic polar measure $a_i\mu_i^{\mathrm{at}}$ by a finite atomic measure with rational coefficients and mass at most $a_iA_i$. Such approximations converge in total variation. Their curve models in background $a_i\eta_i$ have finite rational logarithmic coefficients. Together with the unit coefficient of $D$, these clear denominators in local product monomials. They therefore give analytic models in $\mathcal S_a$ converging to every ideal model. Conversely, if $\Phi_a(q)$ is analytic, restrict to each active curve section with all other potentials finite and with the $Z$ coordinate outside $D$. By \cref{prop:analytic-type-calculus}, its genuine restriction $a_iq_i$ plus a constant is analytic. This is an ordinary model in the positive background $a_i\eta_i$, so \cref{prop:analytic-envelope-type} makes it its ideal projection. The preceding lemma now makes $\Phi_a(q)$ an ideal model. Closedness gives the claimed analytic closure. This argument uses rational coefficients of the actual scaled poles; an irrational $a_i$ need not preserve an analytic template model.

For a common active set, the normalized tuple is determined by the potential. The displayed bounds for $T_{ba}$ follow by comparing each positive weight $b_i$ with $a_i$; the coordinatewise atomic projection commutes with this correspondence.

Finally, normalized compactness in \cref{thm:normalized-compactness} uniformly bounds the $L^1(C_i)$ norms of all $q_i$. Integration along the compact fibers of $\pi_i\circ p$ gives constants $K_i$ bounding their pulled-back $L^1(M)$ norms. Off the pole sets, which have smooth volume zero, the difference of the two potentials is the sum of $(a_i-b_i)q_i\circ\pi_i\circ p$. The triangle inequality proves the uniform estimate, including at zero coefficients. Taking $b=0$ gives uniform convergence to $\Psi$; the diameter formula gives the metric collapse on precisely this restricted locus.
\end{proof}

Thus loss of the auxiliary coefficient leaves the metric in \cref{cor:nonclosed-separated-product-metric} fixed, whereas loss of a curve coefficient removes its coordinate and changes the diameter. These computations do not identify a metric on unrestricted higher-dimensional ordinary models.

\Needspace{18\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{Comparing bounded model spaces}
\label{subsec:weighted-product-metric-comparison}

The weighted spaces can be compared even when their active sets differ. They are bounded, but a positive curve coefficient already prevents compactness. We state the comparison through metric embeddings so that no compactness hypothesis is needed.

\Needspace{14\baselineskip}
\begin{definition}\label{def:bounded-gh-comparison}\index{Gromov--Hausdorff comparison!bounded model spaces}
For nonempty subsets $E,F$ of a metric space $(W,d_W)$, their Hausdorff distance is
\[
 d_{\mathrm H}^W(E,F)=\max\left\{
 \sup_{x\in E}\inf_{y\in F}d_W(x,y),
 \sup_{y\in F}\inf_{x\in E}d_W(x,y)\right\}.
\]
For nonempty bounded metric spaces $E,F$, put
\[
 d_{\mathrm{GH}}(E,F)
       =\inf_{W,\iota,\jmath}d_{\mathrm H}^W(\iota(E),\jmath(F)),
\]
where $\iota,\jmath$ are isometric embeddings into a common metric space $W$. A correspondence is a subset $R\subset E\times F$ whose two coordinate projections are surjective. Its distortion is
\[
 \operatorname{dis}R
       =\sup_{(x,y),(x',y')\in R}
              |d_E(x,x')-d_F(y,y')|.
\]
\end{definition}

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:bounded-metric-correspondence}
For any correspondence $R$ between nonempty bounded metric spaces,
\[
 \frac12|\operatorname{diam}E-\operatorname{diam}F|
       \leq d_{\mathrm{GH}}(E,F)
       \leq\frac12\operatorname{dis}R.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Choose $\varepsilon>0$ with $2\varepsilon\geq\operatorname{dis}R$. Give the disjoint union $E\sqcup F$ its original distances within each space, and use the symmetric cross distance
\[
 D(x,y)=\inf_{(x_0,y_0)\in R}
       \bigl(d_E(x,x_0)+\varepsilon+d_F(y_0,y)\bigr),
       \qquad x\in E,\ y\in F.
\]
It is finite and at least $\varepsilon$. The triangle inequalities involving one cross distance follow from the triangle inequality within the corresponding space and the infimum. For two cross distances, take $(x_0,y_0),(x_1,y_1)\in R$. The distortion bound gives
\[
 \begin{split}
 d_E(x,x')
 &\leq d_E(x,x_0)+d_F(y_0,y_1)
                    +2\varepsilon+d_E(x_1,x')\\
 &\leq d_E(x,x_0)+d_F(y_0,y)+\varepsilon
       +\varepsilon+d_F(y,y_1)+d_E(x_1,x').
 \end{split}
\]
Taking the two infima gives $d_E(x,x')\leq D(x,y)+D(y,x')$. The same argument with $E,F$ exchanged treats the other case. Hence $D$ is a metric extending both metrics. Each point has a correspondent at cross distance at most $\varepsilon$, so its Hausdorff comparison is at most $\varepsilon$. Letting $\varepsilon$ decrease to half the distortion proves the upper bound, including zero distortion.

For the lower bound, suppose two isometric images have Hausdorff distance $h$. For every $\delta>0$, two points in the first image have points in the second within $h+\delta$. Their distance is at most $\operatorname{diam}F+2h+2\delta$. Taking the supremum and then $\delta\downarrow0$ gives $\operatorname{diam}E\leq\operatorname{diam}F+2h$. Exchanging the images and taking the infimum over embeddings proves the lower bound.
\end{proof}

Write $\mathcal I_a\subset\mathcal S_a$ for the ideal subspace in \cref{prop:weighted-product-polar-metric}, with the restricted metric $\rho_a$.

\Needspace{19\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:weighted-product-metric-comparison}\index{Model potential!coefficient comparison}
For all nonnegative coefficient vectors $a,b$, including different active sets,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-product-gh-bounds}
 \left|\sum_i(a_i-b_i)A_i\right|
 \leq d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal S_a,\mathcal S_b)
 \leq\sum_i|a_i-b_i|A_i.
\end{equation}
The same bounds hold for $d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal I_a,\mathcal I_b)$. If $b_i\leq a_i$ for every $i$, both comparisons equal $\sum_i(a_i-b_i)A_i$. In particular, the comparison with the singleton $\mathcal S_0=\mathcal I_0=\{\Psi\}$ equals $\sum_i a_iA_i$.

For $b\leq a$, the map $T_{ba}\Phi_a(q)=\Phi_b(q)$ is a surjective $1$-Lipschitz map and commutes with ideal projection. Every fiber has diameter
\[
 2\sum_{\{i:a_i>0,\ b_i=0\}}a_iA_i.
\]
If some $a_i>0$, both $\mathcal S_a$ and $\mathcal I_a$ fail to be totally bounded and are therefore noncompact.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Use the correspondence
\[
 R_{ab}=\{(\Phi_a(q),\Phi_b(q)):q_i
                \text{ are normalized ordinary curve models}\}.
\]
Every potential in either locus has such a tuple, with arbitrary normalized models in its inactive coordinates, so both projections are surjective. For any two tuples, put $d_i=\|\mu_i-\nu_i\|_{\mathrm{TV}}$. By \eqref{eq:weighted-product-polar-metric}, the difference of their two distances is $\sum_i(a_i-b_i)d_i$, with $0\leq d_i\leq2A_i$. Thus $\operatorname{dis}R_{ab}\leq2\sum_i|a_i-b_i|A_i$. The preceding lemma gives the upper bound in \eqref{eq:weighted-product-gh-bounds}. The diameter formula in \cref{prop:weighted-product-polar-metric} gives the lower bound.

Restrict the tuple correspondence to ideal curve models. It still projects onto both ideal subspaces, and the same distortion bound holds. Atomic measures $A_i\delta_{x_i}$ and $A_i\delta_{y_i}$ attain their full diameters, so the lower bound also agrees. When $b\leq a$, the upper and lower bounds coincide, including when $b=0$.

For the transition map, every active coordinate of $b$ is active for $a$. Uniqueness of the normalized active tuple therefore makes the map well defined. Every $b$-tuple extends to an $a$-tuple, so the map is surjective, and $\sum_i b_i d_i\leq\sum_i a_i d_i$ proves the Lipschitz bound. Coordinatewise atomic projection commutes with discarding and rescaling coordinates. In a fiber, every coordinate with $b_i>0$ is fixed; the discarded active coordinates vary freely. Their diameter is the displayed sum, by the curve diameter calculation.

Finally, fix an active coordinate $i$ and all other coordinates at their zero models. Let $q_i$ have polar measure $A_i\delta_x$, with $x$ ranging over infinitely many distinct points of $C_i$. The resulting potentials are ideal models, and any two are at distance $2a_iA_i$. An infinite separated set excludes total boundedness in both spaces. Thus the comparison above concerns these complete bounded spaces without asserting compactness.
\end{proof}
