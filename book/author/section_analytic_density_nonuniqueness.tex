\section{A real analytic density}
\label{uni-sec:examples}

The last construction prescribes a globally real analytic density before the background is chosen. The density is a polynomial in a real analytic function on projective space. We first choose a symmetric pair of perturbations, depending only on $r$, whose determinants equal that density, and then extend the construction across the projective ends by patching coefficients while preserving the determinant. The two hypersurfaces on which the density vanishes quadratically still separate regions where the difference of the two solutions takes different extremal values.

\Needspace{21\baselineskip}
\begin{theorem}\label{uni-thm:analytic-example}\index{Zero set!real analytic density}
On $X=\mathbb P^3$, with block homogeneous coordinates $[A:B]$ and Fubini--Study form $\omega_X$, put
\[
 t=\frac{|A|^2}{|A|^2+|B|^2},\qquad
 a=\frac1{1+\mathrm{e}},\qquad b=\frac{\mathrm{e}}{1+\mathrm{e}},\qquad
 f_*(t)=(t-a)^2(t-b)^2.
\]
There exist a
smooth nonclosed Hermitian form $\omega$ and distinct smooth
$\omega$-plurisubharmonic functions $u,v$, normalized by $\sup_Xu=\sup_Xv=0$, with
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:analytic-example}
 (\omega+\ddc u)^3=(\omega+\ddc v)^3=f_*(t)\omega_X^3.
\end{equation}
Both solution forms are positive away from the two connected real analytic
hypersurfaces $\{t=a\}$, $\{t=b\}$, and have complex rank two on them.
The density is real analytic on $X$, and it vanishes exactly to second order
in the direction transverse to these hypersurfaces.  For every $n\geq4$ the same assertions hold on
$\mathbb{P}^3\times\mathbb{P}^{n-3}$, with the pulled-back density and the product
Fubini--Study volume, and with rank $n-1$ on the two product
hypersurfaces.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We use the forms $P,Q,R$ and the function $r$ of \cref{subsec:numa-rank-directions}. By \eqref{eq:numa-cube}, the construction reduces to finding scalar coefficients with a prescribed product. By \eqref{eq:numa-fs},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:analytic-fs-cube}
 \omega_X^3=6(\sigma')^2P\wedge Q\wedge R,
 \qquad \sigma'=\sigma(r)\sigma(-r)>0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Prescribing the determinant}
\label{subsec:analytic-prescribed-determinant}

On $U$, one has $t=\sigma(r)$. We first construct two triples of coefficients, positive on the middle band, with product $f_*(\sigma)(\sigma')^2$. At each endpoint of the band, exactly one of the coefficients of $P$ and $Q$ will vanish.

Put $d_*(r)=f_*(\sigma(r))(\sigma'(r))^2$.  Since
$a=\sigma(-1)$, $b=\sigma(1)$, define the positive smooth divided
differences
\[
 k_-(r)=\int_0^1\sigma'(-1+s(r+1))\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad
 k_+(r)=\int_0^1\sigma'(1+s(r-1))\,\mathrm{d}s,
\]
and $H_*(r)=k_-(r)k_+(r)\sigma'(r)>0$.  The fundamental theorem of
calculus gives
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:prescribed-det}
 \sigma(r)-a=(r+1)k_-(r),\quad
 \sigma(r)-b=(r-1)k_+(r),\quad
 d_*=(1-r^2)^2H_*^2.
\end{equation}
These formulas show that $H_*$ is smooth and positive also at the endpoints,
without any division by a vanishing function.  The symmetry
$\sigma(-r)=1-\sigma(r)$ makes $d_*$, and hence $H_*$, even.

Choose the function
\[
 p(r)=
 \begin{cases}\mathrm{e}^{-1/(1-r^2)},&|r|<1,\\0,&|r|\geq1,
 \end{cases}
 \qquad q=p'.
\]
All its derivatives vanish at $r=\pm1$: each derivative is a finite sum
of polynomials in $r$ times powers of $(1-r^2)^{-1}$, multiplied by the exponential, and the
exponential factor dominates every such power.  On $|r|<1$,
$q=-2rp/(1-r^2)^2$.  Define
\begin{align}
 z_b&=\sqrt{q^2+2H_*^2},\qquad
 S_* =\sqrt{r^2+4p^2+2z_b(1-r^2)^2},\label{uni-eq:baseline1}\\
 x_b&=(S_*-r)/2,\qquad y_b=(S_*+r)/2.\label{uni-eq:baseline2}
\end{align}
\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
Both radicands are strictly positive everywhere, so these formulas define
smooth functions on $\mathbb R$, including at the endpoints.  They satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:baseline-identities}
 z_b>|q|,\qquad x_b-y_b=-r,\qquad
 x_by_b=p^2+\frac{z_b}2(1-r^2)^2.
\end{equation}
The coefficients $x_b,y_b$ are nonnegative, positive away from the
endpoints, with values $(1,0)$ at $-1$ and $(0,1)$ at $1$.
On the open band,
\[
 S_*^2-(2p+|r|)^2=2(1-r^2)^2(z_b-|q|)>0,
\]
so $x_b>p$, $y_b>p$.  Consequently both triples
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:twotriples}
 (x_b-p,y_b+p,z_b-q),\qquad (x_b+p,y_b-p,z_b+q)
\end{equation}
are strictly positive there.

We now verify that the two triples have the same product.  Set
$D_*=x_by_b-p^2$, $E_*=p(x_b-y_b)=-rp$.
Equations \eqref{uni-eq:baseline-identities} and the formula for $q$ give
\[
 D_*=(z_b/2)(1-r^2)^2,\qquad qD_*=z_bE_*.
\]
The two products in \eqref{uni-eq:twotriples} therefore equal
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:det-cancellation}
 (D_*+E_*)(z_b-q)=(D_*-E_*)(z_b+q)
       =\frac{D_*}{z_b}(z_b^2-q^2)=d_*.
\end{equation}
For $|r|\geq1$, $p=q=0$ and the same identity reads
$x_by_bz_b=d_*$.  Thus both triples have exactly the prescribed
product, and at the endpoints exactly one of their entries vanishes.

\subsection{Extension over the projective ends}
\label{subsec:analytic-global-patch}

The coefficients just constructed have the right product but need not extend across the projective ends. Away from the zero levels of $f_*(t)$, we interpolate the coefficients of $P$ and $Q$ to those of $f_*(t)^{1/3}\omega_X$, and determine the coefficient of $R$ by the prescribed product.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
Choose a smooth even cutoff $0\leq\eta\leq1$, equal to one on
$|r|\leq3/2$ and zero on $|r|\geq2$.  On $|r|<3/2$ set
$(x,y,z)=(x_b,y_b,z_b)$.  On $|r|>5/4$, where
$f_*(\sigma(r))>0$, put
\begin{align*}
 \kappa&=f_*(\sigma(r))^{1/3},\\
 x&=\eta x_b+(1-\eta)\kappa\sigma(r),\\
 y&=\eta y_b+(1-\eta)\kappa\sigma(-r),\qquad z=d_*/(xy).
\end{align*}
These coefficients are smooth and positive on the outer region.  On the
overlap $5/4<|r|<3/2$, we have $\eta=1$, $p=q=0$ and
$x_by_bz_b=d_*$, so the two definitions agree.  For
$|r|\geq2$ they equal
$\kappa(\sigma(r),\sigma(-r),\sigma'(r))$.  Thus
\[
 \omega_0=xP+yQ+zR
\]
extends smoothly across both omitted projective lines, being equal near them
to $f_*(t)^{1/3}\omega_X$, which is smooth and positive there since
$f_*(0)=f_*(1)=a^2b^2>0$.  On $X$, the form $\omega_0$ is semipositive,
positive away from $r=\pm1$, and of rank two on these levels.

The function
\[
 F(r)=2\int_0^r p(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
\]
is constant on each of the intervals $(-\infty,-1]$ and $[1,\infty)$, and so $F(r)$ extends to a smooth nonconstant function on
$X$.  By \eqref{eq:numa-chain}, half of its complex Hessian is
$H_0=\ddc F/2=p(P-Q)+qR$.  By
\eqref{uni-eq:det-cancellation} and \eqref{eq:analytic-fs-cube}, the forms
$A_0=\omega_0-H_0$, $B_0=\omega_0+H_0$ satisfy
\[
 A_0^3=B_0^3=f_*(t)\omega_X^3
\]
on $U$, and hence on $X$.  Their ranks and positivity
were determined above, after \eqref{uni-eq:twotriples}.  The construction is
symmetric: $x(-r)=y(r)$, and $z$ is even.

\subsection{A positive equation background}
\label{subsec:analytic-repair}

As in the first example, an exact correction makes the average form strictly positive without changing the two solution forms. Here the coefficient of $R$ is already positive, and the correction restores the missing coefficient of $P$ or $Q$ at each endpoint. Let $\varepsilon>0$ be a parameter, restricted below.

Let $h(r)=(r^2-1)\eta(r)$, extend it by zero on the tails, and set
$\rho=\varepsilon h$.  The coefficients of
$\omega=\omega_0+\ddc\rho$ are
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:repair}
 (x+\varepsilon h',\ y-\varepsilon h',\ z+\varepsilon h'').
\end{equation}
On $|r|\leq3/2$, we have $h'=2r$ and $h''=2$.
For $0\leq r\leq3/2$, $x+2\varepsilon r>0$, since the only zero of
$x$ there is at $r=1$, where the correction is positive.
On $[-3/2,0]$, $x$ has a positive minimum $m_0$, so positivity
holds if $3\varepsilon<m_0$.  Reflection proves positivity of the
second coefficient, and the third is $z+2\varepsilon>0$.
On the compact transition region $3/2\leq|r|\leq2$, all original
coefficients have a common positive lower bound $m_1$.  If
$M_1$ bounds $|h'|+|h''|$, taking also
$\varepsilon<m_1/(M_1+1)$ preserves their positivity there.
For $|r|\geq2$, the background equals
$f_*(t)^{1/3}\omega_X>0$.  Hence $\omega$ is strictly positive on $X$.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
The correction is exact, so $\mathrm{d}\omega=\mathrm{d}\omega_0$.  At $r=0$, where $x=x_b$ and $y=y_b$,
evenness of $S_*$ gives $x'(0)=-1/2$ and $y'(0)=1/2$.  Hence
\eqref{eq:numa-differential} gives
\[
 \mathrm{d}\omega_0=(z_b(0)+1/2)\,\mathrm{d}r\wedge(Q-P)\ne0.
\]
Indeed, if $\mathrm{d}r\wedge(Q-P)=0$, wedging with $P$ and $\dc r$
would contradict $P\wedge Q\wedge R>0$.  Thus the Hermitian background $\omega$
is not closed.

Now put $\widetilde u=-F/2-\rho$ and
$\widetilde v=F/2-\rho$.  Then
$\omega+\ddc\widetilde u=A_0$,
$\omega+\ddc\widetilde v=B_0$.
Subtracting their respective suprema gives $u,v$ with $\sup_Xu=\sup_Xv=0$, and \eqref{uni-eq:analytic-example} holds.  Their difference is nonconstant because $F$
is nonconstant.

\subsection{Zeros and higher dimensions}

It remains to examine the regularity of the density and the geometry of its zero levels. Taking a product with projective space then preserves the equality of the Monge--Amp\`ere measures and gives examples in every higher dimension.

The function $t$ is real analytic in every affine chart of $X$, being a
quotient of real polynomials with positive denominator.  Hence $f_*(t)$
is real analytic on $X$.  Its two zero levels are regular hypersurfaces because
$t=\sigma(r)$, $\sigma'>0$, and $\mathrm{d}r\ne0$.
Each level is connected, since it is the quotient of the product of two
three-spheres of fixed nonzero radii by the diagonal action of the circle.
Each level is compact and has zero volume.  The divided
differences in \eqref{uni-eq:prescribed-det} show near the two levels that
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:quadratic-zero}
 f_*(\sigma(r))=(r+1)^2g_-(r),\qquad
 f_*(\sigma(r))=(r-1)^2g_+(r),
\end{equation}
respectively, with smooth strictly positive functions $g_\pm$.

For $n\geq4$, let $\theta$ be the Fubini--Study form on
$Y=\mathbb{P}^{n-3}$, let $\pi_1\colon X\times Y\to X$ and $\pi_2\colon X\times Y\to Y$ be the projections, and set, on $X\times Y$,
\[
 \omega_n=\pi_1^*\omega+\pi_2^*\theta,\qquad
 \Omega_n=\pi_1^*\omega_X+\pi_2^*\theta,\qquad
 u_n=\pi_1^*u,\quad v_n=\pi_1^*v.
\]
In the expansion of an $n$-fold product, only the terms with exactly three factors pulled back from $X$
survive.  Consequently
\[
 (\omega_n+\ddc u_n)^n
 =\binom n3\pi_1^*(A_0^3)\wedge\pi_2^*(\theta^{n-3})
 =f_*(\pi_1^*t)\Omega_n^n,
\]
and the same holds for $v_n$.  On the two product
levels, the solution forms have rank $2+(n-3)=n-1$.  Restriction to a slice $X\times\{y\}$ recovers
$\mathrm{d}\omega\ne0$, so $\omega_n$ is not closed.  Normalization,
distinctness and real analyticity are preserved by pullback.
\end{proof}

Write $\operatorname{Vol}$ for the volume with respect to $\omega_X^3$, or $\Omega_n^n$ in dimension $n$. The quadratic zeros complete the proof of the sharpness assertion in
\cref{uni-thm:threshold}.  In finitely many tubular charts around the zero levels, the density
is bounded above and below by positive multiples of the square of a real
transverse coordinate, and the volume form has a bounded positive
density with respect to coordinate volume.  Away from these charts, the density is bounded below by a positive constant.  Thus, for $s>0$ and $\alpha>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:sharp-asymptotic}
 \operatorname{Vol}\{f_*\leq s\}\asymp\sqrt s\quad(s\downarrow0),
 \qquad f_*^{-\alpha}\in L^1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha<\tfrac12.
\end{equation}
Thus, for every $0<\alpha<1/2$ and every dimension $n\geq3$, there are smooth examples of nonuniqueness whose density $f$ satisfies $f^{-\alpha}\in L^1$; this condition is weaker than the hypothesis $f^{-1/2}\in L^1$ of \cref{uni-thm:threshold}.

\begin{remark}
For this example, the positive-density set $\{f_*(t)>0\}$ has three components: the middle band and the two outer regions. The two solution forms have the same missing direction on each zero level, so $E_{\rm unif}=\{f_*(t)>0\}$. The maximum and the minimum of the difference of the two potentials are attained on the two outer components, whose closures are disjoint, in accordance with \cref{uni-thm:anchors}. Although the closure of the middle component meets both outer closures, the extremal values do not propagate through the middle component.
\end{remark}
