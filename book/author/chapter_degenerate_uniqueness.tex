\chapter{Uniqueness for degenerate equations}
\label{chap:degenerate-uniqueness}

Let $X$ be a connected compact complex manifold of dimension $n\geq1$, let $\omega$ be a Hermitian form, and let $\mathrm{d}V$ be a smooth positive volume form. The preceding chapter gives uniqueness of the normalization constant. We now ask when the corresponding potential is unique up to an additive constant.

For real potentials $u,v$, of the regularity stated in each result, set
\[
 A=\omega+\ddc u,\qquad B=\omega+\ddc v,\qquad w=u-v,
 \qquad T=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A^j\wedge B^{n-1-j}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{uni-eq:linearization}
 \ddc w\wedge T=A^n-B^n.
\end{equation}
When $A,B\geq0$, the polarized cofactor $T$ is semipositive. It may be degenerate and need not be closed. For Sobolev or $C^{1,1}$ potentials, coefficient products in these identities are understood almost everywhere.

For $C^{1,1}$ solutions with the same continuous density, strict positivity of the density supplies local uniform ellipticity. The main issue is to locate the extrema of $w$ in these elliptic regions and to decide whether their values propagate across the zero set. A positive weight making $T$ $\ddc$-closed gives another route: a global energy identity forces the derivative of $w$ to vanish in the cofactor directions. For bounded potentials, an energy measure replaces this pointwise calculation.

If the background has invariant mass, ordinary comparison proves bounded uniqueness for $L^p$ densities, $p>1$, without a connectedness condition on the positive-density set. For a general Hermitian background, however, two zero hypersurfaces can separate regions carrying different extremal values. The projective examples below first place this freedom in an open zero-density band, then retain it with only quadratic zeros, even for a real analytic density. They show why smallness of the zero set alone does not restore uniqueness.
