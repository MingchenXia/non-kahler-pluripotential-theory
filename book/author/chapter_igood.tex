\chapter{Multiplier-ideal envelopes and \texorpdfstring{$\mathcal I$}{I}-goodness}
\label{chap:hermitian-igood}\index{I-goodness@$\mathcal I$-goodness}

Throughout this chapter, $X$ is a connected compact complex manifold of dimension $n$ and $\theta$ is a smooth real $(1,1)$-form, which need not be closed. We fix a smooth positive probability volume form $\mathrm{d}V$ on $X$ and take $L^1$ norms with respect to it. For $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$, $\mathrm{MA}_\theta(u)$ denotes the extended non-pluripolar Monge--Amp\`ere measure $\mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_\theta(u)$ of \cref{thm:arbitrary-extended-ma}; its total mass may be infinite. We write $u\preceq v$ and $u\sim v$ for the ordinary singularity order and equivalence of \cref{def:singularity-order}, and $u\preceq_{\mathcal I}v$ and $u\sim_{\mathcal I}v$ for inclusion and equality of the multiplier ideals $\mathcal I(ku)$ and $\mathcal I(kv)$ at every weight $k>0$, as in \cref{sec:psh-singularity-types}. Finally, $P_\theta[u]$ and $P_{\theta,F}[u]$ denote the ordinary singularity envelopes of \cref{def:singularity-envelope}, below zero and below a smooth obstacle $F$, and $V_\theta=P_\theta(0)$ is the potential with minimal singularities of \cref{sec:obstacle-envelopes}.

Multiplier ideals record weighted holomorphic integrability. Two potentials can have the same multiplier ideals at every weight although their ordinary envelopes differ, so passing from the ordinary singularity type of a potential to its multiplier-ideal class loses information. Analytic approximation makes this loss visible: along dyadic Demailly approximations, the envelopes converge to the greatest nonpositive representative of the multiplier-ideal class, which may exceed the ordinary envelope of the original potential.

In the K\"ahler theory, equality of these two envelopes is also characterized by a mass condition. In a nonclosed background the equality remains meaningful even when the potential has infinite mass, and we therefore study both envelopes along analytic approximations. Examples on curves show that this still does not give continuity of mass or completeness, because diffuse polar curvature can disappear in the limit. The same examples explain why the obstacle has to be taken into account when the background changes.

\section{The K\"ahler characterization}

First suppose that $X$ is K\"ahler, that $\theta$ is a smooth closed big real $(1,1)$-form, and that $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$ has positive non-pluripolar mass. Let $P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$ be the multiplier-ideal envelope of \cref{def:hermitian-i-envelope} below, and let $d_S$ be the singularity pseudometric of \cite[Section~6.2]{SGPT}. The characterization of $\mathcal I$-good singularities in \cite[Theorem~7.1.1]{SGPT} states that the following three conditions are equivalent:
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 u\text{ lies in the }d_S\text{-closure of analytic singularities},\\
 \int_X\mathrm{MA}_\theta(u)
  =\int_X\mathrm{MA}_\theta(P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}),\\
 P_\theta[u]=P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}.
 \end{gathered}
\]
The last condition involves only envelopes. It therefore offers a starting point for the Hermitian problem even where the metric and the mass characterizations are unavailable.

The integral on the right of the middle condition is the \emph{multiplier-ideal volume} of \cite{SGPT}. The upper and lower volumes of \cref{def:singularity-volumes}, by contrast, vary the potential within its ordinary singularity type. The distinction matters in a nonclosed background, where even a bounded change of the potential can alter its total mass.

\section{Ideal projection}

We return to the general setting fixed at the beginning of the chapter. The multiplier-ideal envelope is the greatest nonpositive potential in a multiplier-ideal class. Taking a supremum over the class could a priori enlarge the multiplier ideals; the increasing-limit form of strong openness rules this out, and the envelope is then a projection onto the class.

\begin{definition}\label{def:hermitian-i-envelope}\index{Envelope!multiplier ideal}\index{Multiplier ideal!envelope}
For $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$ define
\[
 P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}
 :=\left(\sup\{v\in\PSH(X,\theta):v\leq0,
                   \ v\sim_{\mathcal I}u\}\right)^*.
\]
\end{definition}

As for ordinary envelopes, the members of the defining family are called \emph{competitors}. The family is nonempty because it contains $u-\sup_Xu$; bounded perturbations do not change multiplier ideals. Since it is bounded above by zero, its regularized supremum is a $\theta$-plurisubharmonic function. The envelope depends only on the multiplier-ideal class of $u$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:hermitian-i-projection}\index{Envelope!ideal projection}
For every $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$,
\[
 P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}\sim_{\mathcal I}u,\qquad
 P_\theta[P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}]_{\mathcal I}=P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}.
\]
The regularization in \cref{def:hermitian-i-envelope} does not change the pointwise supremum. Moreover,
\[
 \sup_X P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}=0.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $q=P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$. By Choquet's lemma, some sequence from the defining family has regularized supremum $q$. Replacing its terms by successive finite maxima, we obtain by \cref{lem:hermitian-i-calculus} an increasing sequence $v_j$ that remains in the family and converges to $q$ almost everywhere.

Fix $x\in X$ and $\lambda>0$. On a coordinate ball about $x$, choose one smooth function $r$ such that every $v_j+r$ and $q+r$ is plurisubharmonic; this is possible because all these functions have complex Hessian bounded below by the same smooth form $-\theta$. The increasing-limit assertion of \cref{thm:local-i-inputs} gives
\[
 \mathcal I(\lambda(q+r))_x
   =\mathcal I(\lambda(v_j+r))_x
   =\mathcal I(\lambda u)_x
\]
for all large $j$; the second equality holds because smooth additions do not change multiplier ideals. Hence $q\sim_{\mathcal I}u$.

Since $q\leq0$, it belongs to its own defining family. The pointwise supremum is therefore at least $q$, and it is at most its regularization $q$; this proves the assertion about regularization. The defining families for $P_\theta[q]_{\mathcal I}$ and $P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$ coincide, which proves idempotence. Finally, if $\sup_Xq<0$, then $q-\sup_Xq$ would be a larger competitor, which is impossible.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:p-below-i}\label{cor:ordinary-preserves-i}
For every $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$,
\[
 u-\sup_Xu\leq P_\theta[u]\leq P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}\leq V_\theta.
\]
Moreover,
\[
 \begin{gathered}
 P_\theta[u]\sim_{\mathcal I}u,\qquad
 P_\theta[P_\theta[u]]_{\mathcal I}=P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I},\\
 P_\theta[P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}]=P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}.
 \end{gathered}
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $h\leq0$ be a competitor for the ordinary envelope, so that $h\preceq u$. The function $\max(h,u-\sup_Xu)$ is nonpositive, ordinarily equivalent to $u$, and dominates $h$. Since ordinarily equivalent potentials have the same multiplier ideals, it is a competitor for the ideal envelope. Taking regularized suprema gives $P_\theta[u]\leq P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$; the two outer inequalities follow directly from the defining families.

Projection and monotonicity of local integrability now give, for every $\lambda>0$,
\[
 \mathcal I(\lambda u)
 \subseteq\mathcal I(\lambda P_\theta[u])
 \subseteq\mathcal I(\lambda P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I})
 =\mathcal I(\lambda u).
\]
Thus the ordinary envelope preserves all multiplier ideals, and it has the same ideal envelope as $u$. For $q=P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$, the inequalities already proved and idempotence give $q\leq P_\theta[q]\leq P_\theta[q]_{\mathcal I}=q$.
\end{proof}

We call $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$ \emph{normalized} if $\sup_Xu=0$. We call $u$ an \emph{ordinary model} if $P_\theta[u]=u$, that is, if $u$ is an envelope fixed point in the sense of \cref{def:singularity-envelope}, and an \emph{ideal model} if $P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}=u$. Models of either kind are normalized, and by the last identity in \cref{prop:p-below-i} every ideal model is an ordinary model. The map $u\mapsto P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$ is called \emph{ideal projection}.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:i-envelope-finite}
If $\theta$ is semipositive and big, then $P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$ has finite extended Monge--Amp\`ere mass for every $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By the last identity in \cref{cor:ordinary-preserves-i}, $P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$ is an ordinary model, that is, an envelope fixed point. The mass estimate for envelope fixed points in \cref{prop:fixed-point-finite} applies.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{14\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:i-envelope-operations}
For $u,v\in\PSH(X,\theta)$,
\[
 u\preceq_{\mathcal I}v\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}\leq P_\theta[v]_{\mathcal I}.
\]
For $a>0$ one has $P_{a\theta}[a u]_{\mathcal I}=aP_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$. For smooth real $(1,1)$-forms $\theta_i$ and potentials $u_i\in\PSH(X,\theta_i)$, $i=1,2$,
\[
 P_{\theta_1}[u_1]_{\mathcal I}+P_{\theta_2}[u_2]_{\mathcal I}
 \leq P_{\theta_1+\theta_2}[u_1+u_2]_{\mathcal I}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $h$ be a competitor for $P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$. Since $h\sim_{\mathcal I}u\preceq_{\mathcal I}v$, the Lelong-number criterion in \cref{thm:local-i-inputs} shows that $\max(h,v-\sup_Xv)$ is multiplier-ideal equivalent to $v$. This function is nonpositive and dominates $h$, so it is a competitor for $P_\theta[v]_{\mathcal I}$; taking the supremum over $h$ proves that the envelope preserves the order. Multiplication by $a$ is a bijection between the defining families in the scaling identity. For the sum, $P_{\theta_1}[u_1]_{\mathcal I}+P_{\theta_2}[u_2]_{\mathcal I}$ is nonpositive and $(\theta_1+\theta_2)$-plurisubharmonic, and it is multiplier-ideal equivalent to $u_1+u_2$ by projection and \cref{lem:hermitian-i-calculus}. It is therefore a competitor for the envelope on the right.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Analytic and divisorial singularities}

For a potential $a$ with analytic singularities, the multiplier-ideal order controls the ordinary order: by \cref{lem:analytic-ideal-dominance}, every quasi-plurisubharmonic $v$ with $v\preceq_{\mathcal I}a$ satisfies $v\preceq a$. The ideal envelope of $a$ is then a competitor for its ordinary envelope, and the two envelopes coincide. This extends \cite[Proposition~3.2.10]{SGPT} to smooth backgrounds.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:analytic-envelope-type}\index{Envelope!analytic singularities}
Let $a\in\PSH(X,\theta)$ have analytic singularities. Then
\[
 a\sim P_\theta[a]=P_\theta[a]_{\mathcal I}.
\]
In particular the common envelope has analytic singularities with the same coefficient and ideal as $a$, up to a bounded remainder.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Set $q=P_\theta[a]_{\mathcal I}$. Projection gives $q\sim_{\mathcal I}a$, so \cref{lem:analytic-ideal-dominance} gives $q\preceq a$. Since $q\leq0$, it is a competitor for $P_\theta[a]$. Together with \cref{prop:p-below-i}, this yields
\[
 a-\sup_Xa\leq P_\theta[a]\leq q\leq P_\theta[a].
\]
Hence the two envelopes are equal and ordinarily equivalent to $a$. Since they differ from $a$ by a bounded function, they inherit its analytic presentation.
\end{proof}

The same argument applies to the potential of a countable divisorial current of finite mass, with \cref{lem:divisorial-potential-domination} in place of \cref{lem:analytic-ideal-dominance}.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:divisorial-envelope-equality}
Let $S$ be a closed positive divisorial current of finite mass on a connected compact complex manifold $Z$, and choose a smooth residual decomposition $S=\beta+\ddc v$ as in \cref{cor:divisorial-current-ordinary-type}. For every Hermitian form $\Omega$ with $v\in\PSH(Z,\Omega)$,
\[
 P_\Omega[v]=P_\Omega[v]_{\mathcal I}\sim v.
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Set $q=P_\Omega[v]_{\mathcal I}$. Projection gives $q\sim_{\mathcal I}v$, and \cref{lem:divisorial-potential-domination} gives $q\preceq v$. Thus $q$ is a competitor for $P_\Omega[v]$. Together with \cref{prop:p-below-i}, this yields $v-\sup_Zv\leq P_\Omega[v]=q\preceq v$.
\end{proof}

\section{Changes of background and envelope mass}

A smooth change of representative $(\theta,u)\mapsto(\theta+\ddc f,u-f)$ preserves the current $\theta+\ddc u$ but moves the zero obstacle. The resulting envelopes remain ordinarily equivalent after transport. In a nonclosed background, however, their masses can differ.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:i-envelope-gauge}
Let $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$ and $f\in C^\infty(X,\mathbb R)$. Set $\theta'=\theta+\ddc f$, $u'=u-f$, $m=\min_Xf$ and $M=\max_Xf$. Then
\[
 P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}+m
 \leq P_{\theta'}[u']_{\mathcal I}+f
 \leq P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}+M.
\]
The same inequalities hold for the ordinary envelopes.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If $v$ is a competitor for $P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$, then $v-f+m$ is $\theta'$-plurisubharmonic, nonpositive and multiplier-ideal equivalent to $u'$. Therefore
$v\leq P_{\theta'}[u']_{\mathcal I}+f-m$,
and taking the envelope gives the first inequality. Conversely, a competitor $v'$ in the new background gives the competitor $v'+f-M$ in the old one, which proves the second inequality. For the ordinary envelopes, the same calculation applies with the ordinary singularity order in place of multiplier-ideal equivalence.
\end{proof}

For $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$, put
\[
 W_\theta(u)=\int_X\mathrm{MA}_\theta(P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I})\in[0,+\infty].
\]
This quantity depends on the decomposition of the current, even for smooth potentials.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:raw-i-volume-gauge-failure}
There are two smooth decompositions of the same positive current for which the corresponding values of $W$ are different.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Use the Hermitian form $\omega$ and the smooth function $g=u_t$ of \cref{ex:torus-mass}, with $t\ne0$ sufficiently small. Both $\omega$ and $\omega'=\omega+\ddc g$ are Hermitian, and
\[
 \omega+\ddc0=\omega'+\ddc(-g).
\]
For a smooth potential in a Hermitian background, all multiplier ideals are trivial and zero is a competitor for its multiplier-ideal envelope, so that envelope is zero. Consequently
\[
 W_\omega(0)=\int_X\omega^2,\qquad
 W_{\omega'}(-g)=\int_X(\omega+\ddc g)^2.
\]
By the explicit formula \eqref{eq:torus-mass}, the two integrals differ.
\end{proof}

The underlying torus is K\"ahler; the dependence comes from the chosen nonclosed background. In the closed theory, the invariance of mass under bounded changes of the potential gives the missing step after \cref{prop:i-envelope-gauge}.

Singularity volumes, on the other hand, range over the entire ordinary type, so the bijection of competitors in the gauge change preserves them. Under bounded mass, \cref{prop:model-comparison-gauge} accordingly transports ordinary models of positive volume and preserves their upper-volume comparison.

\Needspace{14\baselineskip}
\subsection{Envelope equality and infinite mass}

For each $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$, the equality
\begin{equation}\label{eq:background-goodness}
 P_\theta[u]=P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}
\end{equation}
is meaningful. We call it \emph{envelope goodness in the fixed background}.\index{I-goodness@$\mathcal I$-goodness!envelope equality} Its relation to mass differs from the K\"ahler characterization: it can hold when the potential has infinite mass while its envelope has finite mass.

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:goodness-infinite-mass}
If a Hermitian metric $\omega$ fails the bounded mass property of \cref{def:bmp}, there exists $U\in\PSH(X,\omega)$ such that
\[
 P_\omega[U]=P_\omega[U]_{\mathcal I}=0,
 \qquad U\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\omega,0),
 \qquad \mathrm{MA}^{\mathrm{ext}}_\omega(U)(X)=+\infty,
\]
where $\mathcal E_{\mathrm{env}}(X,\omega,0)$ is the relative envelope class of \cref{def:relative-envelope-class}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take the potential $U$ of \cref{prop:infinite-envelope-class}, which has the stated ordinary envelope, class membership and infinite mass. Since $P_\omega[U]=V_\omega=0$, \cref{prop:p-below-i} gives $P_\omega[U]_{\mathcal I}=0$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Complete model topologies}

We now compare the complete metrics obtained so far and ask how they might extend to all ordinary models. On a curve, the total variation of polar curvature is a complete metric on ordinary models, ideal projection keeps the atomic part of the polar curvature, and the distance to the closure of the analytic models is exactly the diffuse polar mass, by \cref{cor:curve-model-completeness,prop:curve-analytic-distance-defect}. This topology is stronger than $\tau_{\omega,0}$ on ordinary models: \cref{prop:curve-ordinary-topology-incomplete} exhibits a concentration of diffuse polar curvature that is lost in the latter topology.

Adding the smooth curve background as a further coordinate gives the complete space of \cref{thm:joint-curve-model-completeness}. Its analytic closure is the locus of ideal models, also over backgrounds of zero area, and metric convergence controls the normalized potentials in $L^1$ and in capacity. This construction keeps the positive-volume topology but imposes a different Cauchy condition at the zero-volume boundary.

For a smooth big background with bounded mass, \cref{thm:positive-model-complete-metric} gives a complete topology on ordinary models of positive lower volume. Its locus of ideal models is the completion of the analytic models of positive volume, by \cref{cor:positive-ideal-metric-completion}.

At zero volume, the product constructions of the preceding subsections keep information that top-degree mass cannot detect. A fixed divisor with unit coefficient forces every ordinary representative of the induced models to have zero mass, while polar curvature on the curve factors still distinguishes these models and gives a complete metric whose analytic closure is exactly the locus of ideal models, with ideal projection as the unique nearest-point retraction. These conclusions hold on the product loci of \cref{prop:divisorial-product-polar-metric,prop:general-auxiliary-product-metric}, including the nonclosed background with unbounded ambient mass in \cref{ex:unbounded-auxiliary-polar-metric}.

Changing the curve areas rescales the polar distances, and a zero area removes the corresponding coordinate. The comparisons in \cref{prop:weighted-product-metric-comparison} quantify this change even when different sets of coordinates survive. If the curve backgrounds converge uniformly to forms of area zero, the diameters tend to zero and the potentials converge in $L^1$, uniformly over the model tuples, and the limit still contains the normalized smooth potentials of the limiting backgrounds, by \cref{cor:arbitrary-product-zero-area-collapse}. These loci thus describe explicit degenerations beyond the positive-volume theory.

We do not know how to extend the complete topology to general ordinary models while keeping analytic closure and compatibility across backgrounds. Since polar curvature is the coordinate that achieves this on a curve, it is natural to test polar curvature on all modifications in higher dimension. The rest of this subsection shows that this candidate fails in three ways: the first polar current misses isolated poles unless a modification exposes them; normalizing polar traces by Gauduchon forms makes the topology depend on the chosen forms; and diffuse poles of product type are invisible to polar curvature on every modification, although they lower the volume.

Finally, even the full residual measure on the pole set can miss anisotropy. In \cref{prop:equal-residual-anisotropic-models}, distinct ordinary models of positive volume have the same residual measure, the same singularity volumes, the same multiplier ideals and the same first polar currents on every modification, while their volume comparison is positive. A general extension must therefore record more than these data.

\begin{problem}\label{qu:i-good-no-bmp}
Extend the complete topology of \cref{thm:positive-model-complete-metric} to ordinary models of zero lower volume and to backgrounds without bounded mass, in such a way that the closure of the analytic models is exactly the locus of ideal models. The extension should recover the curve topology of \cref{cor:curve-model-completeness} and the background compatibility of \cref{sec:model-background-comparison,sec:two-sided-model-backgrounds}, including higher-dimensional limits that lose bigness or positive lower volume.
\end{problem}
