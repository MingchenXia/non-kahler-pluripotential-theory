\section{The minimum principle}
\label{sec:psh-minimum}

An infimum of plurisubharmonic functions need not be plurisubharmonic. Kiselman's minimum principle describes an important exception: the infimum over a real parameter $t$ of a family $u_t$ whose complexification $u_{\Real z}$ is plurisubharmonic in the joint variables. Freezing the coefficients of the background form, we show that the principle preserves the exact lower curvature bound $-\theta$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:invariant-strip-convexity}
Let $I\subset\mathbb R$ be a nonempty open interval, set $S_I=\{z\in\mathbb C:\Real z\in I\}$, and let $\pi\colon X\times S_I\to X$ be the projection. Let $(u_t)_{t\in I}$ be a family of functions on $X$ such that $U(x,z)=u_{\Real z}(x)$ is quasi-plurisubharmonic on $X\times S_I$ and $\pi^*\theta+\ddc U\geq0$. For each $x$, the function $t\mapsto u_t(x)$ is finite and convex or identically $-\infty$. The finite alternative holds for almost every $x$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A local smooth correction making $U$ plurisubharmonic can be chosen to depend only on $x$. Hence the restriction of $U$ to $\{x\}\times S_I$ is subharmonic or identically $-\infty$. In the first case its distributional Laplacian is the second derivative of $t\mapsto u_t(x)$, which is therefore nonnegative. The resulting convex representative is finite on $I$, and by the submean inequality it agrees everywhere with the subharmonic function. Finally, $U$ is locally integrable on the product, and Fubini's theorem, applied on products of coordinate balls with a fixed rectangle in $S_I$, excludes the identically $-\infty$ alternative for almost every $x$.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:radial-subharmonic-convexity}
Let $I\subset\mathbb R$ be a nonempty open interval. A radial function $z\mapsto q(\log|z|^2)$ is subharmonic on the annulus $\{z:\log|z|^2\in I\}$ if and only if $q$ is finite and convex on $I$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The holomorphic covering map $z=\mathrm{e}^{w/2}$ pulls the function back to $q(\Real w)$, and the argument of \cref{lem:invariant-strip-convexity} proves necessity. Conversely, a finite convex function $q$ has nonnegative distributional second derivative. Thus $q(\Real w)$ is subharmonic, and subharmonicity descends through the local biholomorphisms of the covering.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:hermitian-kiselman}\index{Kiselman minimum principle}
Under the hypotheses of \cref{lem:invariant-strip-convexity}, the function
\[
 q(x)=\inf_{t\in I}u_t(x)
\]
is either $\theta$-plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty$. If $I$ is bounded, then $q\in\PSH(X,\theta)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The classical local Kiselman principle states that if a plurisubharmonic function on the product of a domain with a strip does not depend on the imaginary part of the strip variable, then its infimum over the real part is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty$. The interval of the real part may be unbounded. We use this local result from \cite{SGPT}.

Fix $x_0\in X$, choose local holomorphic coordinates centered at $x_0$, and let $\kappa$ be their Euclidean form. Fix $\eta>0$. On a sufficiently small coordinate ball the constant form $\beta=\theta(x_0)$ satisfies
\[
 \beta-\eta\kappa\leq\theta\leq\beta+\eta\kappa.
\]
Choose a quadratic function $r$ with $\ddc r=\beta+\eta\kappa$. Then $U+r\circ\pi$ is plurisubharmonic on the product of the ball with $S_I$. Its infimum over the parameter is $q+r$, so the local principle shows that $q+r$ is plurisubharmonic or identically $-\infty$ on the ball; in the first case
\[
 \ddc q\geq-\beta-\eta\kappa\geq-\theta-2\eta\kappa.
\]
In a fixed atlas, the Euclidean forms are bounded by a fixed multiple of $\omega$, so letting $\eta\downarrow0$ proves the exact lower bound $\ddc q\geq-\theta$. Since a plurisubharmonic function cannot be identically $-\infty$ on a nonempty open set, the local alternative propagates across overlapping balls on the connected manifold $X$. Thus either $q\in\PSH(X,\theta)$ or $q\equiv-\infty$. No regularization is needed, because an infimum of upper semicontinuous functions is upper semicontinuous.

Now suppose that $I$ is bounded. By \cref{lem:invariant-strip-convexity}, the function $t\mapsto u_t(x)$ is finite and convex for almost every $x$, and a supporting affine function at any point of $I$ bounds it from below on the bounded interval $I$. Hence $q(x)>-\infty$ almost everywhere, which excludes the alternative $q\equiv-\infty$.
\end{proof}

For example, the proposition applies to $u_t-t\tau$ for a fixed real number $\tau$. On an unbounded interval the infimum may be identically $-\infty$; this alternative will encode the cutoff of a test curve (\cref{def:hermitian-envelope-testcurve}).

\subsection{Pointwise Legendre transforms}\label{sec:psh-legendre}

The minimum principle can also show that a supremum is already upper semicontinuous. For a concave family of potentials, the regularized Legendre transform is a potential on a product. The minimum principle identifies its inverse transforms with the original family everywhere, and applying the one-variable duality once more identifies the regularization with the pointwise supremum. We first state this duality precisely, including its behavior at the endpoints; the general Legendre--Fenchel theorem is recalled in \cite[Appendix~A]{SGPT}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:halfline-legendre-duality}\index{Legendre duality}
Let $c\in\mathbb R$. Suppose $g\colon\mathbb R\to[-\infty,0]$ is upper semicontinuous, concave and decreasing, finite somewhere, and equal to $-\infty$ on $(c,\infty)$. Then
\[
 f(t)=\sup_{\tau<c}\bigl(g(\tau)+t\tau\bigr),\qquad t>0,
\]
is finite and convex, satisfies $f(t)\leq ct$, and obeys
\[
 g(\tau)=\inf_{t>0}\bigl(f(t)-t\tau\bigr),\qquad
 \lim_{t\downarrow0}f(t)=\sup_{\tau<c}g(\tau).
\]
Conversely, every finite convex $f$ on $(0,\infty)$ with $f(t)\leq ct$ is recovered by the first formula from $g(\tau)=\inf_{t>0}(f(t)-t\tau)$, and satisfies the same endpoint identity. The formulas also hold for $g\equiv-\infty$, with $f\equiv-\infty$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $g$ is decreasing and finite somewhere, it is finite on a half-line $(-\infty,\tau_1)$. Hence the defining supremum is finite for $t>0$: it is bounded above by $ct$ and below by any one of its finite terms. Concavity and upper semicontinuity give $g(c)=\lim_{\tau\uparrow c}g(\tau)$, so including $\tau=c$ does not change the supremum. The Legendre transform $t\mapsto\sup_\tau(g(\tau)+t\tau)$ of $-g$ is infinite for $t<0$. At $t=0$ it equals $m=\sup_{\tau<c}g(\tau)$, and the bounds
\[
 g(\tau)+t\tau\leq f(t)\leq m+ct
\]
show that $f(t)\to m$ as $t\downarrow0$. The biconjugacy theorem for the closed convex function $-g$ therefore gives the inverse formula; the value at $t=0$ may be omitted without changing the infimum.

Conversely, start with $f$. At each $t>0$, choose the slope $p$ of a supporting line of $f$. The upper bound $f(s)\leq cs$ as $s\to\infty$ forces $p\leq c$. If $p<c$, the supporting line gives $g(p)=f(t)-tp$, so the first formula recovers $f(t)$. If $p=c$, then $f(s)-cs$ is constant for $s\geq t$, because this function is convex and bounded above, hence decreasing, while its supporting line at $t$ is horizontal. Consequently, for every $\tau<c$,
\[
 f(t)-ct\leq g(\tau)\leq f(t)-t\tau,
\]
and letting $\tau\uparrow c$ again recovers $f(t)$. In particular, $g$ is finite somewhere. As an infimum of affine functions with slopes $-s<0$, it is upper semicontinuous, concave and decreasing. Letting $s\downarrow0$ in the bound $f(s)-s\tau\leq s(c-\tau)$ gives $g\leq0$, and letting $s\to\infty$ gives $g=-\infty$ for $\tau>c$. The endpoint identity now follows from the first part. The case of functions identically equal to $-\infty$ follows directly from the formulas.
\end{proof}

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
Fix $c\in\mathbb R$, and let $\Gamma_\tau\in\PSH(X,\theta)$ for $\tau<c$ be functions with $\Gamma_\tau\leq0$ such that $\tau\mapsto\Gamma_\tau(x)$ is concave and decreasing for every $x$. Extend the family to $\tau=c$ by the decreasing limit $\Gamma_c=\lim_{\tau\uparrow c}\Gamma_\tau$, and by $\Gamma_\tau\equiv-\infty$ for $\tau>c$. Define the forward transform of the family by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hermitian-legendre-forward}
 U_t(x)=\sup_{\tau<c}\bigl(\Gamma_\tau(x)+t\tau\bigr),
 \qquad t>0.
\end{equation}
\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
For a family $(u_t)_{t>0}$, the inverse transform is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hermitian-legendre-backward}
 \widehat u_\tau(x)=\inf_{t>0}\bigl(u_t(x)-t\tau\bigr).
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:testcurve-closed-fibers}
At each $x$, the extended function $\tau\mapsto\Gamma_\tau(x)$ is upper semicontinuous and concave, or identically $-\infty$. Its forward transform satisfies
\[
 \Gamma_\tau(x)=\inf_{t>0}\bigl(U_t(x)-t\tau\bigr)
 \qquad(\tau\in\mathbb R).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix $\tau<c$ and let $\tau_j\uparrow\tau$. The decreasing limit of the functions $\Gamma_{\tau_j}$ dominates $\Gamma_\tau$, so it belongs to $\PSH(X,\theta)$. Choose $r\in(\tau,c)$. Outside the pole set of $\Gamma_r$, the concave function $s\mapsto\Gamma_s(x)$ is finite near $\tau$ and hence continuous there. Thus the decreasing limit equals $\Gamma_\tau$ almost everywhere, and hence everywhere by \cref{lem:qpsh-almost-everywhere-order}. This proves left continuity at every $\tau<c$; at $c$ it holds by definition. Since the function is decreasing, left continuity gives upper semicontinuity, also where the value is $-\infty$, and concavity survives the extension. The inverse formula is \cref{lem:halfline-legendre-duality} at each $x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:hermitian-legendre-usc}
For the preceding family $(\Gamma_\tau)_{\tau<c}$, the pointwise supremum in \eqref{eq:hermitian-legendre-forward} is already upper semicontinuous on $X\times(0,\infty)$. Its complexification $U(x,z)=U_{\Real z}(x)$ is quasi-plurisubharmonic on $X\times\{\Real z>0\}$ and satisfies $\pi^*\theta+\ddc U\geq0$, where $\pi\colon X\times\{\Real z>0\}\to X$ is the projection. Each slice belongs to $\PSH(X,\theta)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Each term $(x,z)\mapsto\Gamma_\tau(x)+\tau\Real z$ of the supremum satisfies the current inequality on the product, since $\Real z$ is pluriharmonic. The bound $\Gamma_\tau\leq0$ gives $U_t\leq ct$, while any fixed $\tau_0<c$ gives the minorant $\Gamma_{\tau_0}+t\tau_0$. By the local upper-envelope theorem, as in \cref{sec:obstacle-envelopes}, the upper semicontinuous regularization $V$ of $U$ on the product is quasi-plurisubharmonic, satisfies $\pi^*\theta+\ddc V\geq0$, and agrees with $U$ almost everywhere. It does not depend on $\operatorname{Im}z$ and satisfies $V_t\leq ct$. The pointwise supremum $U$ is measurable, since by the left continuity in \cref{lem:testcurve-closed-fibers} the parameters may be restricted to rational numbers.

By Fubini's theorem, there is a set $Z\subset X$ of zero smooth volume, containing the pole set of $\Gamma_{\tau_0}$, such that $U_t(x)=V_t(x)$ for almost every $t>0$ whenever $x\notin Z$. For such $x$, both sides are finite convex functions of $t$, by \cref{lem:halfline-legendre-duality,lem:invariant-strip-convexity}, so by continuity the equality holds for every $t>0$.

For each $\tau<c$, \cref{prop:hermitian-kiselman} gives
\[
 \chi_\tau=\inf_{t>0}(V_t-t\tau)
 \in\PSH(X,\theta)\cup\{-\infty\}.
\]
Off $Z$ it equals $\Gamma_\tau$ by \cref{lem:testcurve-closed-fibers}. In particular, $\chi_\tau$ is not identically $-\infty$, and \cref{lem:qpsh-almost-everywhere-order} gives $\chi_\tau=\Gamma_\tau$ everywhere. At each $x$, the convex function $t\mapsto V_t(x)$ can be recovered from these inverse transforms by \cref{lem:halfline-legendre-duality}, also when it is identically $-\infty$. We obtain
\[
 V_t(x)=\sup_{\tau<c}\bigl(\chi_\tau(x)+t\tau\bigr)=U_t(x).
\]
Finally, the minorant $\Gamma_{\tau_0}+t\tau_0$ shows that no slice $U_t$ is identically $-\infty$, so every slice belongs to $\PSH(X,\theta)$.
\end{proof}
