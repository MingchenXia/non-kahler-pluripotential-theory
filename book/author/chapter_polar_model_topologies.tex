\chapter{Polar curvature and zero-volume models}
\label{chap:polar-model-topologies}

Two kinds of complete topologies on ordinary models are available so far. On a curve, the total variation of polar curvature is a complete metric on ordinary models, ideal projection keeps the atomic part of the polar curvature, and the distance to the closure of the analytic models is exactly the diffuse polar mass, by \cref{cor:curve-model-completeness,prop:curve-analytic-distance-defect}. This topology is stronger than the topology $\tau_{\omega,0}$ of \cref{sec:ordinary-type-topology} on ordinary models: \cref{prop:curve-ordinary-topology-incomplete} exhibits a concentration of diffuse polar curvature that is lost in the latter topology. Adding the smooth curve background as a further coordinate gives the complete space of \cref{thm:joint-curve-model-completeness}. Its analytic closure is the locus of ideal models, also over backgrounds of zero area, and metric convergence controls the normalized potentials in $L^1$ and in capacity. This construction keeps the positive-volume topology but imposes a different Cauchy condition at the zero-volume boundary. In higher dimension, for a smooth big background with bounded mass, \cref{thm:positive-model-complete-metric} gives a complete topology on ordinary models of positive lower volume. Its locus of ideal models is the completion of the analytic models of positive volume, by \cref{cor:positive-ideal-metric-completion}.

This chapter studies ordinary models in higher dimension outside the positive-volume theory. If a potential is the sum of a logarithmic potential with fixed divisorial poles and the pullback of a potential from the base of a submersion, both its envelopes reduce to envelopes on the base, and every representative of the induced ordinary types has zero top-degree mass. Equality of the two envelopes below every smooth obstacle, the question of \cref{subsec:all-obstacle-topology}, then reduces to the base as well. On products with curves, these zero-volume models keep information that top-degree mass cannot detect. A fixed divisor with unit coefficient forces every ordinary representative of the induced models to have zero mass, and the volume comparison vanishes on them. Polar curvature on the curve factors nevertheless distinguishes these models and gives a complete metric whose analytic closure is exactly the locus of ideal models, with ideal projection as the unique nearest-point retraction. These conclusions hold on the product loci of \cref{prop:divisorial-product-polar-metric,prop:general-auxiliary-product-metric}, including the nonclosed background with unbounded ambient mass in \cref{ex:unbounded-auxiliary-polar-metric}.

Changing the curve areas rescales the polar distances, and a zero area removes the corresponding coordinate. The comparisons in \cref{prop:weighted-product-metric-comparison} quantify this change even when different sets of coordinates survive. If the curve backgrounds converge uniformly to forms of area zero, the diameters tend to zero and the potentials converge in $L^1$, uniformly over the model tuples, and the limit still records the normalized smooth potentials of the limiting backgrounds, by \cref{cor:arbitrary-product-zero-area-collapse}. These loci thus describe explicit degenerations beyond the positive-volume theory.

A curve quotient can also describe the entire model space. If $p\colon X\to C$ is a surjective holomorphic map with connected fibers, including critical fibers, and $\beta$ is a smooth real $(1,1)$-form of nonnegative area on the compact curve $C$, every potential in the background $\theta=p^*\beta$ descends to $C$. For $\dim X\geq2$ all top-degree masses vanish, but the polar trace, normalized by the constant Gauduchon volume of the fiber divisor with its multiplicities, gives the complete curve metric on all ordinary $\theta$-models by \cref{fibration:polar-model-metric}. The ideal models form the closure of the analytic models, and \cref{fibration:all-obstacle-criterion} characterizes equality of the ordinary and ideal envelopes below every smooth obstacle. For an arbitrary surjective map to a compact curve, \cref{curve-map:component-factor} gives the finite factor used in \cref{fibration:finite-curve-factor}, so disconnected fibers have the same complete model-space description. This description holds even on a threefold without the bounded mass property, by \cref{fibration:unbounded-hopf-threefold}. The polar trace must be kept upstairs: in \cref{fibration:disconnected-polar-pushforward}, two distinct analytic models have the same trace pushforward to the original base curve. The last section shows that polar curvature on all modifications does not extend these descriptions to general ordinary models in higher dimension.

Throughout the chapter, compact complex manifolds are connected, and the bounded mass property is not assumed; mass bounds, expressed through the lower and upper masses $v_\pm$ of \cref{def:bmp}, are imposed only where they are stated. We keep the notation of \cref{chap:hermitian-igood}. For a smooth real $(1,1)$-form $\theta$ and $u\in\PSH(X,\theta)$, $\mathrm{MA}_\theta(u)$ is the extended non-pluripolar measure of \cref{thm:arbitrary-extended-ma}, whose total mass may be infinite; $\preceq$, $\sim$, $\preceq_{\mathcal I}$ and $\sim_{\mathcal I}$ are the ordinary and multiplier-ideal orders; and $P_{\theta,F}[u]$ and $P_{\theta,F}[u]_{\mathcal I}$ are the ordinary and ideal envelopes below a continuous obstacle $F$ (\cref{def:singularity-envelope,def:hermitian-i-envelope,subsec:obstacle-transport}), written $P_\theta[u]$ and $P_\theta[u]_{\mathcal I}$ when $F=0$. A potential is normalized if its supremum is zero, and ordinary and ideal models are the fixed points of the two envelopes. We also use the singularity volumes of \cref{def:singularity-volumes}, the relative envelope classes of \cref{def:relative-envelope-class}, and the volume comparison $d_\theta$ and the sets $\mathcal M_\delta(\theta)$ of \cref{def:model-volume-comparison}, where the comparison is used only when the upper volumes involved are finite. On curves we use the polar curvature of \cref{sec:local-polar-parts} and the classification of models in \cref{sec:curve-zero-volume-models,sec:curve-model-backgrounds}.

\section{Polar currents on modifications}
\label{sec:polar-currents-modifications}

We do not know how to extend the complete topology to general ordinary models while keeping analytic closure and compatibility across backgrounds. Since polar curvature is the coordinate that achieves this on a curve, it is natural to test polar curvature on all modifications in higher dimension. This section shows that this candidate fails in three ways: the first polar current misses isolated poles unless a modification exposes them; normalizing polar traces by Gauduchon forms makes the topology depend on the chosen forms; and diffuse poles of product type are invisible to polar curvature on every modification, although they lower the volume.

Finally, even the full residual measure on the pole set can miss anisotropy. In \cref{prop:equal-residual-anisotropic-models}, distinct ordinary models of positive volume have the same residual measure, the same singularity volumes, the same multiplier ideals and the same first polar currents on every modification, while their volume comparison is positive. A general extension, as asked for in \cref{qu:singularity-topology-program}, must therefore record more than these data.
