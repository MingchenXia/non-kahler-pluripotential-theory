\section{Homogeneous sections on a Hopf manifold}

Let $n\geq2$ and let $z=(z_1,\ldots,z_n)$ be the coordinates on $\mathbb C^n$. Set
\[
 X_n=(\C^n\setminus\{0\})/\langle z\mapsto \mathrm{e}^{-1}z\rangle.
\]

The scalar contraction imposes an exact homogeneity condition on lifted sections. It reduces their dimension to a monomial count, while the invariant metric makes the kernel constant. The resulting growth is at most $m^{n-1}$, reflecting the dimension of the projective base.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
On the covering space put
\[
 s=\log|z|^2,\qquad \alpha=\ddc s,\qquad
 \zeta=\mathrm{d}s\wedge\dc s,\qquad \omega=\alpha+\zeta.
\]
The contraction changes $s$ by $-2$, so the displayed forms descend to $X_n$. The calculation in \cref{chap:hopf}, with $\zeta$ in place of its symbol $\beta$, gives
\[
 \alpha^n=0,\qquad \zeta^2=0,\qquad
 \ddc\zeta=-\alpha^2,
 \qquad \ddc(\omega^{n-1})=-(n-1)\alpha^n=0.
\]
Thus $\omega$ is Gauduchon. Under projectivization $\pi:X_n\to\mathbb P^{n-1}$, the form $\alpha$ is the pullback of the normalized Fubini--Study form. The elliptic fiber calculation in \cref{chap:hopf} gives
\[
 \int_{X_n}\alpha^{n-1}\wedge\zeta=2,
 \qquad \int_{X_n}\omega^n=2n.
\]
Set $\mathrm{d}V=\omega^n/(2n)$, a probability measure.

For an integer $k\geq0$ let $L_k$ be the line bundle defined by
\[
 (z,t)\sim(\mathrm{e}^{-1}z,\mathrm{e}^{-k}t)
\]
on $(\C^n\setminus\{0\})\times\C$. Give it the metric
$|t|_{h_k}^2=|t|^2|z|^{-2k}$. Both factors transform inversely, so this metric descends. Its curvature is $k\alpha$. Apply the section and kernel constructions above with $L=L_k$, $h=h_k$ and this probability volume.

\Needspace{15\baselineskip}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:hopf-bergman-exact}\index{Bergman kernel!homogeneous Hopf example}
For $m\geq1$, with weight $u=0$, we have
\[
 N_m:=h^0(X_n,L_k^m)=\binom{km+n-1}{n-1},
 \qquad B_{m,0}=N_m.
\]
The Bergman potential is the constant $m^{-1}\log N_m$, and its curvature current is $k\alpha$. Moreover,
\[
 \deg_\omega L_k=2k(n-1),\qquad
 \lim_{m\to\infty}m^{-n}N_m=0,
 \qquad (k\alpha)^n=0.
\]
In contrast, $\int_{X_n}\omega^n=2n>0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A section lifts to a holomorphic function $f$ on $\C^n\setminus\{0\}$ satisfying
\[
 f(\mathrm{e}^{-1}z)=\mathrm{e}^{-km}f(z).
\]
Since $n\geq2$, Hartogs' removable-singularity theorem extends $f$ to all of $\C^n$. In its Taylor series, a monomial of total degree $j$ transforms by $\mathrm{e}^{-j}$. Comparing coefficients shows that only degree $km$ occurs. Conversely every homogeneous polynomial of that degree has the required equivariance. Counting monomials gives the binomial coefficient, including the constant polynomial when $k=0$.

The inner product defined by $h_k^m$ and $\mathrm{d}V$ is invariant under $U(n)$, so the basis-independent function $K_{m,0}$ is $U(n)$-invariant. It is also unchanged under any positive radial dilation: every section is homogeneous of degree $km$, and the factor $|z|^{-2km}$ in its squared norm cancels that dilation. Unitary transformations and positive radial dilations act transitively on $\C^n\setminus\{0\}$. Consequently $K_{m,0}$ is constant. Its integral is $N_m$ by the orthonormal-basis identity, and $\mathrm{d}V$ has mass one. Thus $K_{m,0}=B_{m,0}=N_m$.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
The potential and curvature statements follow immediately. Finally,
\[
 \int_{X_n}k\alpha\wedge\omega^{n-1}
 =k(n-1)\int_{X_n}\alpha^{n-1}\wedge\zeta
 =2k(n-1).
\]
The binomial coefficient grows at most as $m^{n-1}$, proving the normalized limit, and $\alpha^n=0$ proves the vanishing curvature mass.
\end{proof}

For $n=3$ and $k>0$, these positive-degree line bundles have quadratic section growth on the Gauduchon threefold of \cref{thm:hopf-unbounded}. The unrestricted Hermitian masses on that same manifold are unbounded. For every $n\geq2$ and $k>0$, their projective complements have least smooth convexity index two by \cref{ex:hopf-exact-complement-index}, with $q=\mathrm e^{-1}$ and $a=\mathrm e^{-k}$. When $k=0$, the line bundle is trivial and its complement has least smooth convexity index $n+1$ by the same example.
