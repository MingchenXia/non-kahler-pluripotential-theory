## Foundations

Chapter 1 develops distributional local correction, the elementary calculus, isolated-puncture extension in dimension at least two with a fixed smooth background, periodic subharmonic rigidity, smooth conformal-current regularity, ordered analytic-support rigidity, normalized compactness, exponential integrability, local products, bounded smooth-background products, ordered contact with fixed positive factors and pullback by holomorphic submersions, Hermitian capacity and its volume/sublevel estimates, regularization, multiplier ideals, divisorial estimates, global smooth-residual potentials and canonical divisorial ordinary types, obstacle envelopes, the minimum principle and pointwise Legendre duality, pullback and descent, convergence against a fixed non-pluripolar measure, bounded-carrier integrability and coordinate smoothing of measure data. The analytic regularization with a prescribed multiplier-ideal type is kept separate from the decreasing sequence with smooth resolved remainders. The Chern regularization estimates retain their different boundedness and background hypotheses.

Chapter 2 proves the smooth and bounded mass-invariance criterion directly after its mass polynomial. Chapters 2–4 explain torsion, mass variation, the obstruction to a primitive of the raw Monge–Ampère one-form, and Gauduchon traces and positivity. Chapter 3 defines the radial energy once for all smooth backgrounds and computes its closed and nonclosed first variations. Chapter 4 passes from conserved traces to the positive-current criterion, attained thresholds over the closure of smooth Gauduchon tests, and smooth adjoint compatibility. Its bounded adjoint extension uses the Chapter 2 mass identity and Chapter 1 capacity continuity. A Gauduchon surface has invariant smooth mass, but this alone does not give the raw energy primitive. Persistence under potential perturbation in higher dimensions requires the precise mass-invariance identities.

Chapter 5 identifies normalized potentials with their finite Gauduchon traces, describes admissible trace data by a scalar Green inverse and introduces a complete trace metric. Its linear inverse L1 estimate and square-root forward estimate apply to unbounded potentials and have sharp exponents. The nonlinear mass and capacity counterexamples occur with their constructions in Chapters 6 and 15.

Chapter 6 constructs non-pluripolar measures for every smooth background from positive restricted products. It passes bounded ordered contact to finite levels, proves weighted lower semicontinuity with varying quasi-continuous weights, and gives the unbounded density and linear-trace inequalities. For a Hermitian background, finiteness for every potential is equivalent to uniform bounded mass. Failure produces infinite mass with integrable Hessian coefficients and all exponential integrability, and smooth prescribed masses with strong linear convergence. Infinite determinant integrals can coexist with coefficient-L1 convergence of currents and total-variation convergence of their traces.

Chapter 7 derives finite weighted measures and capacity continuity from one decreasing-weight criterion, with explicit exponential, polynomial and critical logarithmic estimates. Uniform tails characterize an increasing integrability bound and give undamped convergence; the two mass-concentration examples show why they matter.

Chapter 8 constructs the Hopf counterexample by varying the heat time and correcting the resulting small positivity defect on the base. Its sharp fiber-average inequality gives minimum mass six and identifies every bounded minimizer as a base pullback. These conclusions concern the explicit Hopf geometry.

## Equations and singularity types

Chapter 9 derives smooth solvability from scalar and potential bounds, ellipticity and adjoint compatibility, and then studies scalar variation. For the Gauduchon ansatz, a single cofactor identity links the torsion calculation, largest-eigenvalue estimate, gradient rescaling and variation. Chapter 10 passes from capacity estimates to uniform compactness, constructs normalized and exponential continuous solutions, and separates the unique scalar limit from cluster potentials. Its Hopf example distinguishes uniform convergence of potentials from weak convergence of densities. Chapter 11 develops extremum propagation, cofactor energy and invariant-mass comparison before constructing smooth projective examples with separating zero-density sets. Keep a unique scalar, a unique potential and a selected approximation limit distinct. For a smooth nonnegative density with connected dense positive set, Section 11.4 proves uniqueness of bounded normalized solutions by level energies and concentrating test potentials. Its continuity input follows from the proper equation in Chapter 10. The same energy argument also proves uniqueness for bounded measurable density with a positive essential lower bound. Positivity almost everywhere alone remains insufficient.

Chapter 12 applies the first-chapter envelope tools to contact sets, domination, extended volumes and relative envelope classes. General stretching and finite rooftop arguments retain arbitrary smooth backgrounds; model positivity and the stronger comparison statements retain their own assumptions.

Chapter 13 develops prescribed-singularity equations without a blanket metric mass hypothesis. It uses Chapter 6 weighted lower semicontinuity for extended measures and develops measure representations, equations in a fixed ordinary type, and minimal-type Lp equations for arbitrary smooth big backgrounds. Global Lp data, p>1, supply the local Holder input for interior smoothness on the strict-barrier locus. Smooth density on an arbitrary open set alone does not supply that integrability. The local boundary examples explain this distinction. The chapter also treats individual-density thresholds and exponential approximations.

Chapter 14 develops the scalar functional, supporting probabilities and their generic uniqueness, convex duality, measurable stability, entropy bounds, reference comparison and vanishing-parameter selection. Its selection theorem identifies the whole vanishing-exponent limit for fixed nonzero Lp data, p > 1, in ordinary minimal type on a smooth big background. Sections 14.8–14.10 compare positive backgrounds uniformly over all bounded tests, identify ordinary divisorial equations with positive-residual equations, and prove full-potential stability for moving sections in the stated regimes. On closed big model references with small unbounded locus and positive mass, the functional retains exactly mass. Closed and nonclosed examples show that identical functionals and supports do not determine the singularity type or potential limit. Chapter 15 develops ideal projection and ordinary-envelope comparison, analytic and divisorial equality, background changes and their mass obstruction, and the ideal L1 metric and its curve completion before ordered envelope convergence and dyadic analytic approximation. Fixed-weight ideal inclusions imply the convergence criterion; a curve example rules out L1 and divisorial convergence alone. The curve counterexamples show that this metric does not control non-pluripolar mass. The scalar-distance comparison is made in a Kähler background. Section 29.8 proves analytic approximation and its converse for positive-volume ordinary models under bounded mass. Section 29.9 proves smooth-gauge invariance after ordinary projection and uniform comparison under vanishing background increases. Its analytic criterion permits vanishing positive curvature error; corrected ordinary models converge in the original background. Section 29.10 constructs model transport under two-sided small background changes, proves uniform comparison and independence of the strict reference, and extends the analytic criterion to these varying backgrounds. Section 29.11 gives a triangle-inequality counterexample for the volume formula and constructs a complete compatible metric on all positive-lower-volume ordinary models, with the ideal models as the metric completion of the analytic ones. The extension to zero lower volume or infinite mass and background degeneration remain open here.

Chapter 16 groups subgeodesic calculus with interior continuity and joint endpoint bounds, constructs Perron segments with explicit barriers and time control, and compares the smooth product equation with the torsion terms in the L2 action. The general strip convexity, minimum and gluing principles are used from Chapter 1. Chapter 17 proves the envelope correspondence through finite-interval Perron replacement and rooftop comparison, using the general pointwise Legendre results from Chapter 1. Dominated convergence gives the initial limit, and the explicit logarithmic-pole envelope follows from a punctured-disc maximum principle. Chapter 18 constructs finite additive trace data from restricted multiplier ideals, using the general analytic-restriction and modification lemmas in Chapter 1. Its combined calculus precedes curve realization; ordinary domination identifies the curve ideal envelopes, and the product formula separates exact trace data from representative mass. On a curve, Green potentials realize these data and the source singularity topology is equivalent to total variation of the atomic measures. Restricted analytic envelopes converge in L1 to the trace envelope in the original restricted background; the dyadic version is decreasing. On every compact modification, the envelope of the inherited data in the pulled-back background equals the pulled-back envelope. Vanishing Hermitian perturbations and restricted analytic envelopes on that model converge to it. A sufficiently large Hermitian background gives positive finite-mass models. When the target submanifold is Kahler, a sufficiently large closed background realizes every trace number on every compact modification, as does every larger Hermitian background. The ambient manifold need not be Kahler. Curve fibrations also give exact non-Kahler realizations. Chapter 29 also realizes all trace orders when the target has bounded mass and positive lower mass, using sufficiently large Hermitian backgrounds. Realization outside these regimes and ordinary-type comparison under arbitrary background changes remain open.

## Geometry and section spaces

Chapter 19 develops weighted section spaces together with their Bergman currents, Gauduchon degree obstructions, a bounded section algebra and the exact curve growth formula. Residual effective divisors and normalized compactness give the restricted-trace obstruction in every dimension. Its Hopf and infinite-mass examples separate section growth from Hermitian mass. Chapter 20 combines flag valuations with their Gauduchon bounds, then counts graded sections by intrinsic lattice volume, including degree periodicity and zero-dimensional bodies. One section-ratio-field argument identifies the growth dimension and supplies the full-dimensional Moishezon consequence. Point blowups supply flags on every compact complex manifold; full-dimensional growth implies a projective modification and bounded mass. Chapter 21 identifies weighted sections and Hermitian measures on the Hopf fibration with their base counterparts. The converse positivity argument makes its mass bound apply to every fiber-constant Hermitian potential. It derives the reduced Lp equation and the logarithmic gradient simplex. Chapter 22 constructs finite-mass divisorial trace currents on every compact modification and their Bott–Chern classes. Constant-trace smooth representatives give continuity of positive-current pullback; exceptional divisors measure the change in the canonical ordinary types. Chapter 23 uses affine classes with a prescribed exterior derivative to retain the torsion of a nonclosed background. Analytic singularities have nef Cartier realizations; the general approximation changes the derivative by a vanishing smooth error. One residual family can contain both finite and infinite raw masses. Chapter 24 constructs compatible ideal-envelope systems and their canonical decreasing retraction. Recovery is equivalent to preservation of every slice's multiplier ideals. A ruled surface exhibits an extra limiting divisorial pole as the residual volume tends to zero; raw Hermitian mass also fails smooth-gauge invariance. Linear compatibility on modifications does not imply continuity of nonlinear mass or realization of every proposed trace system.

Chapter 25 applies the Chapter 2 mass-invariance criterion to density independence and entropy, and characterizes smooth mass extrema on threefolds. It develops Gauduchon variational volume through the normalized smooth equation, its Hessian and derivative, and a compact dual problem over currents. Uniform determinant tails control integrable-density attainment; the general unbounded trace-density inequality is used from Chapter 6. Smooth positive attainment, semipositive attainment away from an analytic set and integrable-current attainment have different regularity consequences. The closed-core nef subtraction theorem gives two fixed initial tests and a finite improper cost, including a nonclosed blowup example. Elliptic-product maximizing sequences have the sharp critical determinant norm while all higher moments diverge. Upper bounded mass and positive lower mass are independent; the elliptic example has finite upper mass and zero lower mass.

Chapter 26 studies partial kernels and quotient equilibrium. General compact-obstacle bounds, smooth-center ideals, logarithmic-pole constructions and modification calculus are used from Chapter 1. Birational transfer uses the canonical Jacobian correction and retains a Kähler modification for convergence; submersion transfer uses fiber probabilities and the base-dimensional normalization. The Hopf example gives a nonzero limit that loses the original singularity type. The isolated-point and horizontal-center calculations are grouped before the exact one-dimensional growing-order model and compact-curve localization in its stated growth range. For horizontal poles over projective space it proves the exact domination envelope, a lower bound in every degree, local uniform and global L1 convergence of partial potentials, and total-variation convergence of kernel measures with the base-dimensional normalization. Arbitrary selected-space approximation and the general Fubini–Study lower bound remain open.

Chapter 27 develops Gauduchon trace compactness, closed Bott–Chern classes and singular loci before restricted masses and divisorial constraints at the maximal subsolution coefficient. Sections 27.6–27.7 give finite restricted masses on Kähler resolutions in class C and residual mass formulas for actual divisorial traces with bounded remainder. Nefness of the residual class follows from its bounded potentials. Positive mass supplies a strict current; the blowup example separates this from preservation of ordinary singularity type. The elliptic example identifies the closed cones and section spaces and computes boundary volume and minimizing-test degeneration. Ample-locus regularity uses global Lp data and the strict analytic barrier. The blowup metric uses the first-chapter puncture extension; the normal-length criterion and torus profiles give the sharp logarithmic completeness exponent. The integrable-pole examples retain their distinction from a prescribed nonintegrable pole and from flow-compatible data. Chapter 28 relates the Lee equation to ordinary and twisted potentials, applies the distinction to the Hopf metric, and groups the coupled LCK volume equations and invariant-volume Vaisman theorem in one section. The Vaisman proof follows from the smooth Hermitian equation, flow invariance and transverse Stokes integration, with metric reconstruction and its scale checked explicitly. The Chern–Ricci-flat Gauduchon rigidity proof uses the squared norm supplied by Bianchi and Bochner. Its Lee–Gauduchon cone is defined on any compact complex manifold; the chapter proves duality and bimeromorphic invariance. A separate final section characterizes strongly Gauduchon metrics by positive exact currents and de Rham mass bounds, and distinguishes these conditions on the Calabi–Eckmann quotient. The temporarily hidden Nakamaye chapter remains excluded.

## Theory under mass bounds and historical sources

Its finite-deficit stretching criterion gives a quantitative Lelong bound on each fixed compact model. The decreasing-model result also allows smooth backgrounds decreasing uniformly in form order to a smooth big limit.
